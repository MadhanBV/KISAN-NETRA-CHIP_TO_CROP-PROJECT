AGRICULTURE KNOWLEDGE CONVERSATION CORPUS A Practical, Farmer-Friendly
Reference for Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)

Document purpose This document is a general agricultural knowledge
corpus intended for indexing in a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)
system. It is written in a conversational question-and-answer style so
that an agricultural assistant can retrieve useful explanations when
farmers ask questions in everyday language.

Important use and safety note This document provides general educational
information, not a substitute for local agricultural extension advice,
laboratory soil testing, veterinary care, official weather warnings,
pesticide labels, or government rules. Agricultural recommendations
depend on crop variety, growth stage, soil, climate, irrigation, pest
pressure, and local regulations. A RAG assistant must ask for missing
context instead of pretending that one recommendation works everywhere.
It must never invent pesticide doses, product approvals, fertilizer
rates, market prices, government benefits, or disease diagnoses. For
chemical products, the current registered product label and advice from
a qualified local agricultural officer take priority. If a farmer
describes severe crop damage, suspected poisoning, livestock illness, or
immediate danger, the assistant should recommend prompt help from the
appropriate local professional or emergency service.

How to use this corpus Treat the conversations as reference material,
not as scripts that must be repeated word for word. Retrieve the most
relevant passages, combine them with the farmer’s crop and location
details, and explain the answer in simple language. If a question
requires current information, such as today’s weather, mandi price,
product availability, scheme eligibility, or pesticide registration,
retrieve that information from a trusted live source. This document does
not contain live data.

============================================================ PART 1.
UNDERSTANDING A FARM AND A FARMER’S QUESTION
============================================================

Conversation: Getting the right details before giving advice

Farmer: My crop is not growing properly. What should I do?

Agricultural Assistant: I can help you think through the problem. First,
please tell me which crop you are growing, where your farm is located,
and how old the crop is. Is the whole field affected, or only a few
plants? Are the leaves yellow, curled, spotted, dry, or unusually small?
Has there been heavy rain, hot weather, waterlogging, or a recent
fertilizer or pesticide application?

Farmer: Why do you need so many details? Can’t you just tell me which
fertilizer to use?

Agricultural Assistant: I understand that you want a quick solution. But
slow growth can have many causes. The crop may need nutrients, or the
roots may be damaged by waterlogging. The soil may be too acidic or
alkaline. There may be a pest, disease, poor seed quality, unsuitable
weather, or irrigation problem. Adding fertilizer without identifying
the cause can waste money and sometimes make the problem worse. Let’s
identify the likely cause first.

Farmer: What information should I keep about my farm?

Agricultural Assistant: Keep a simple farm record. Note the village or
approximate farm location, field area, crop and variety, sowing or
transplanting date, seed source, soil type if known, irrigation source,
fertilizer applications, pest and disease observations, sprays, labour
costs, and harvest quantity. Even a notebook is useful. If you use a
digital application, the same information can be stored there. These
records make future advice more accurate.

Farmer: I do not know the exact soil type or soil nutrients.

Agricultural Assistant: That is common. You can begin with what you
know. Is the soil sandy and loose, sticky when wet, or somewhere in
between? Does water drain quickly or remain in the field? For a reliable
nutrient and pH assessment, arrange a soil test through an appropriate
soil testing laboratory, agricultural department, university, or
authorised service. Visual observation alone cannot accurately measure
nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, or soil pH.

Guidance for the assistant: Ask only the most important follow-up
questions. Do not overwhelm the farmer with a long form. Start with
crop, location, crop age or stage, symptoms, affected area, and recent
weather or farm operations. If the farmer cannot answer every question,
provide cautious general guidance and explain what remains uncertain.

Conversation: Understanding crop growth stages

Farmer: What do you mean by crop growth stage?

Agricultural Assistant: A crop passes through different stages during
its life. Many crops begin with germination or establishment, then
develop leaves and stems, produce branches, flower, form fruits or
grains, fill those fruits or grains, and finally mature. The names and
timing differ between crops. A tomato plant, a paddy crop, a mango tree,
and a groundnut crop do not follow exactly the same schedule.

Farmer: Why does the stage matter?

Agricultural Assistant: Because the crop’s needs change. A young plant
needs successful establishment and healthy roots. During vegetative
growth, it develops leaves and stems. During flowering and fruit
formation, water stress, heat, poor pollination, or nutrient imbalance
can affect yield. During fruit or grain filling, the crop needs suitable
conditions to develop quality and weight. Near harvest, the farmer must
consider maturity, weather, market demand, labour, storage, and
transport.

Farmer: Can the application calculate the crop stage from a photograph?

Agricultural Assistant: A photograph may help estimate visible growth
stage for some crops, but it cannot always determine the exact age or
variety. The system should combine the image with the farmer’s sowing
date, crop type, variety, and field observations. It should show an
estimate and allow the farmer to correct it.

============================================================ PART 2.
SOIL HEALTH AND SOIL TESTING
============================================================

Conversation: Why is soil testing important?

Farmer: My neighbour uses a lot of fertilizer and gets a good harvest.
Should I use the same amount?

Agricultural Assistant: Not necessarily. Two nearby farms can have
different soil conditions, crop varieties, irrigation, and previous
nutrient applications. Applying the same fertilizer amount to every
field may cause waste or nutrient imbalance. A soil test helps
understand the soil’s condition and supports a more suitable nutrient
plan.

Farmer: What does a soil test tell me?

Agricultural Assistant: Depending on the laboratory and test package, it
may report soil pH, electrical conductivity, organic carbon, available
nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and selected secondary or
micronutrients. The report may also include recommendations for a
particular crop. Read the units and interpretation carefully because
results from different methods are not always directly comparable.

Farmer: What is soil pH?

Agricultural Assistant: Soil pH indicates how acidic or alkaline the
soil is. It affects the availability of nutrients to plants and the
activity of soil organisms. Some nutrients become less available when
the pH is too low or too high. Different crops tolerate different pH
ranges, so there is no single ideal value for every crop and soil.

Farmer: If my soil is acidic, should I add lime?

Agricultural Assistant: Lime may be recommended for certain acidic
soils, but the quantity depends on soil test results, soil texture,
crop, and the soil’s buffering capacity. Do not guess the quantity. A
soil laboratory or agricultural specialist can interpret the result and
recommend an appropriate material and application method. Liming is not
a universal treatment for every field.

Farmer: What if the soil is alkaline?

Agricultural Assistant: The correct management depends on why the soil
is alkaline and whether it is also saline or sodic. Water quality,
drainage, soil structure, and the source of salts matter. A soil and
irrigation-water test is useful. Do not apply amendments simply because
someone says the soil is alkaline. Follow a site-specific
recommendation.

Conversation: Soil sampling

Farmer: How should I collect soil for testing?

Agricultural Assistant: First contact the testing laboratory or local
agricultural service for its preferred sampling instructions. In
general, collect several small samples from representative spots in the
same field and combine them into one composite sample. Avoid unusual
spots such as compost heaps, field edges, bunds, water channels,
fertilizer piles, and places where manure was recently concentrated. Use
clean tools and a clean container. The sampling depth depends on the
crop and the laboratory’s protocol. Keep different fields or clearly
different soil zones separate.

Farmer: Can I mix soil from all my fields into one bag?

Agricultural Assistant: It is better not to mix fields that have
different crops, soil types, management histories, or drainage. A single
mixed sample may hide important differences. Label each sample with the
field name, location, crop, date, and any other details requested by the
laboratory.

Conversation: Organic matter and compost

Farmer: What is organic matter, and why do people say it improves soil?

Agricultural Assistant: Organic matter comes from decomposed plant and
animal materials. It can improve soil structure, water-holding capacity,
aeration, and biological activity. It helps soil aggregates remain
stable and can contribute nutrients as it decomposes. The benefit
develops over time; compost is not an instant cure for every crop
problem.

Farmer: Is farmyard manure the same as compost?

Agricultural Assistant: They are related organic inputs, but their
composition and preparation can differ. Well-decomposed farmyard manure,
compost, vermicompost, green manure, and crop residues are all used in
soil management. Their nutrient content varies. Fresh or poorly
decomposed material may create problems, including temporary nutrient
competition, weed seeds, or undesirable organisms. Use properly prepared
material and follow local recommendations.

Farmer: Can I burn crop residues?

Agricultural Assistant: Burning residues can cause air pollution,
destroy useful organic material, and create health and environmental
problems. Where practical, consider options such as chopping and
incorporating suitable residues, composting, mulching, using
residue-management equipment, or other locally recommended uses. The
best method depends on the crop, residue, time available, machinery, and
the next crop.

============================================================ PART 3.
PLANT NUTRIENTS AND FERTILIZER MANAGEMENT
============================================================

Conversation: Understanding NPK

Farmer: I see NPK written on fertilizer bags. What does it mean?

Agricultural Assistant: NPK refers to nitrogen, phosphorus, and
potassium, three major plant nutrients.

Nitrogen supports leaf and stem growth and is part of chlorophyll and
proteins. Too little nitrogen may cause older leaves to become pale or
yellow and can reduce growth. Too much nitrogen can cause excessive
vegetative growth, weak plants, delayed maturity, lodging in some crops,
or greater disease and pest susceptibility.

Phosphorus supports energy transfer, root development, flowering, and
seed formation. Deficiency can restrict growth and may sometimes cause
dark green or purplish leaves, although symptoms vary with crop and
temperature.

Potassium supports water regulation, enzyme activity, stress tolerance,
and crop quality. Deficiency may cause leaf-edge yellowing or scorching
in some crops, but symptoms are not identical across species.

Farmer: Can I identify the exact missing nutrient from leaf colour?

Agricultural Assistant: Leaf symptoms can suggest a nutrient problem,
but they are not proof. Disease, water stress, root damage, pH,
salinity, and several nutrient deficiencies can look similar. Soil
testing, plant tissue testing where appropriate, crop history, and field
inspection provide stronger evidence.

Conversation: Why more fertilizer is not always better

Farmer: If a little fertilizer helps, will a larger amount give me a
larger harvest?

Agricultural Assistant: No. More fertilizer does not automatically mean
more yield. Excess fertilizer can damage roots, increase salt
concentration, cause nutrient imbalance, contaminate water, increase
production costs, and sometimes reduce crop quality. The right amount
depends on crop, yield target, soil test, previous applications,
irrigation, and local recommendations.

Farmer: How should I decide the fertilizer amount?

Agricultural Assistant: Use a crop-specific recommendation based on soil
testing and local agricultural guidance. Check the nutrient content on
the bag, not just the brand name. Different products contain different
percentages of nutrients. Record the amount applied and the date. If a
recommendation is given in kilograms of nutrient per hectare but the
product is sold as a fertilizer blend, the calculation must account for
the product’s nutrient percentage and the field area. Ask a qualified
adviser to verify calculations if you are unsure.

Farmer: Should I apply all fertilizer at once?

Agricultural Assistant: Not always. Some crops benefit from split
applications, especially when nutrients can be lost through leaching,
runoff, or volatilisation, or when crop demand changes through the
season. The correct schedule depends on the crop and local
recommendation. Do not copy a schedule from a different crop or region.

Conversation: Fertilizer application safety

Farmer: What should I remember when applying fertilizer?

Agricultural Assistant: Read the product label and follow the
recommended crop, rate, timing, placement, and precautions. Use clean
measuring equipment. Avoid spilling fertilizer into wells, ponds,
streams, and drainage channels. Do not mix products unless the label or
a qualified adviser confirms compatibility. Store fertilizer in its
original labelled packaging in a dry, secure place, away from children,
animals, food, and drinking water. Wash hands after handling.

Farmer: Can I use fertilizer that has become wet?

Agricultural Assistant: It depends on the product and how it was stored.
Some fertilizers cake or change physically after absorbing moisture. Do
not assume the product is still suitable. Check the label and ask the
supplier or manufacturer. Never use an unidentified or unlabelled
material.

Conversation: Micronutrients

Farmer: What are micronutrients?

Agricultural Assistant: Micronutrients are nutrients plants need in
smaller quantities, but they are still essential. Examples include zinc,
iron, boron, manganese, copper, molybdenum, chlorine, and nickel. A
deficiency can affect growth, flowering, fruit formation, or quality.
However, excess amounts can also be harmful. Use soil or plant test
information and crop-specific advice before applying micronutrient
products.

============================================================ PART 4.
WATER MANAGEMENT AND IRRIGATION
============================================================

Conversation: How often should I irrigate?

Farmer: How many days should I wait between irrigations?

Agricultural Assistant: There is no single irrigation interval for every
crop. It depends on crop type, growth stage, soil texture, rooting
depth, weather, rainfall, irrigation method, and water availability.
Sandy soils generally drain faster than many clay soils, but field
conditions vary. Crops may need different water amounts during
flowering, fruit development, or grain filling.

Farmer: How do I know whether the crop needs water?

Agricultural Assistant: Observe soil moisture in the root zone, crop
appearance, recent rainfall, and weather forecasts. Soil moisture
sensors can help when properly installed and interpreted. A wilted plant
does not always mean the soil is dry; roots may be damaged or
waterlogged. Check the soil before irrigating.

Farmer: Can I irrigate just before heavy rain?

Agricultural Assistant: If useful rainfall is expected, additional
irrigation may be unnecessary. Check the reliability of the forecast and
the current soil moisture. Avoid waterlogging, runoff, and erosion. The
decision should consider the crop’s sensitivity and the amount of rain
expected.

Conversation: Drip irrigation

Farmer: What is drip irrigation?

Agricultural Assistant: Drip irrigation delivers water near the plant
root zone through pipes and emitters. It can improve water-use
efficiency when properly designed and maintained. It may also support
precise nutrient application through fertigation where permitted and
appropriately managed.

Farmer: Is drip irrigation suitable for every farm?

Agricultural Assistant: It is useful for many crops, but suitability
depends on crop spacing, field layout, water quality, filtration needs,
cost, maintenance capacity, and available support. Emitters can clog,
pipes can leak, and uneven pressure can create uneven irrigation. A
system should be designed for the farm rather than purchased only
because it is popular.

Conversation: Waterlogging

Farmer: My field has standing water after rain. What should I do?

Agricultural Assistant: Waterlogging reduces oxygen around roots and can
cause yellowing, wilting, root damage, and increased disease risk. If
safe and feasible, restore drainage using locally suitable field
channels or other recommended methods. Avoid walking or operating heavy
machinery on very wet soil if it may cause compaction. Check whether the
crop has suffered root damage and seek local advice for severe or
persistent waterlogging.

============================================================ PART 5.
WEATHER, CLIMATE AND FARM DECISIONS
============================================================

Conversation: Using weather forecasts

Farmer: The weather application says rain is expected. Can I trust it
completely?

Agricultural Assistant: Forecasts are useful but not perfect. Weather
can vary across short distances, and forecast confidence changes with
time. Use forecasts as decision support, not as a guarantee. Check the
forecast issue time, location, expected rainfall range, and warning
level. Combine it with what you observe in your field.

Farmer: What farm activities can weather affect?

Agricultural Assistant: Weather can affect sowing, transplanting,
irrigation, fertilizer application, pesticide application, flowering,
pollination, harvesting, drying, storage, and transport. Heavy rain can
wash away inputs or damage crops. High winds can affect spraying and
cause lodging or physical damage. Heat can cause flower drop, sunscald,
or water stress in sensitive crops. Humid conditions can favour some
fungal diseases.

Farmer: Should I spray before rain?

Agricultural Assistant: Do not make that decision from a general rule.
The product label specifies rainfastness, application conditions, and
other restrictions. Rain shortly after application may reduce
effectiveness or increase runoff, depending on the product. Wind,
temperature, humidity, pollinator activity, and worker safety also
matter. Follow the label and local expert advice.

Conversation: Heat stress

Farmer: My plants are wilting during very hot afternoons. Is that always
a disease?

Agricultural Assistant: Not necessarily. High temperature and high
evaporative demand can cause temporary midday wilting, especially when
roots cannot supply water quickly enough. Check whether plants recover
in the evening and inspect soil moisture. Persistent wilting, root
damage, vascular disease, salinity, or waterlogging can also cause
symptoms. Do not diagnose from one observation alone.

Farmer: What can I do during a heatwave?

Agricultural Assistant: Follow local crop-specific advisories. Maintain
appropriate soil moisture without overwatering, use suitable mulching
where recommended, avoid unnecessary disturbance, and protect sensitive
nursery plants where practical. Irrigation timing should follow local
guidance and water availability. Do not apply unverified mixtures or
chemicals as a heat treatment.

============================================================ PART 6.
COMMON PESTS AND INTEGRATED PEST MANAGEMENT
============================================================

Conversation: What is integrated pest management?

Farmer: I see insects on my crop. Should I spray immediately?

Agricultural Assistant: Not every insect is harmful. Some insects are
pollinators or natural enemies that feed on crop pests. Integrated Pest
Management, or IPM, combines several methods to manage pests while
reducing unnecessary pesticide use.

Farmer: What are the main steps?

Agricultural Assistant: First, identify the pest correctly. Then monitor
its presence and damage. Check whether the pest level is important
enough to require action. Use suitable cultural, mechanical, biological,
and, when necessary, chemical methods. Evaluate the result and keep
records.

Farmer: What is a beneficial insect?

Agricultural Assistant: Beneficial insects include predators and
parasitoids that naturally reduce pest populations. Ladybird beetles,
lacewings, certain wasps, and other organisms may help control pests.
Their presence is one reason to avoid unnecessary broad-spectrum
pesticide use.

Farmer: What are cultural methods?

Agricultural Assistant: Cultural methods change farm practices to make
conditions less favourable for pests. Examples include crop rotation,
field sanitation, timely sowing, removal of heavily infested plant
material where appropriate, balanced nutrition, resistant varieties,
proper spacing, and management of alternate host plants.

Farmer: What are mechanical methods?

Agricultural Assistant: Mechanical methods physically remove or block
pests. Depending on the crop and pest, these may include hand-picking,
barriers, traps, nets, or removal of affected plant parts. Use methods
that are practical and safe for the crop.

Farmer: What are biological methods?

Agricultural Assistant: Biological control uses natural enemies or
approved biological products to suppress pests. Their effectiveness
depends on correct identification, timing, environmental conditions,
product quality, and compatibility with other practices. Buy biological
products from reliable sources and follow their instructions.

Conversation: Monitoring pests

Farmer: How often should I inspect my field?

Agricultural Assistant: Inspect regularly, with frequency based on crop
stage, pest risk, and local advice. Look at different parts of the field
rather than only the edge. Examine upper and lower leaf surfaces,
growing points, flowers, fruits, and stems as relevant. Record what you
observe and take clear photographs. A consistent monitoring record helps
identify changes over time.

Farmer: What should I record?

Agricultural Assistant: Record the date, field, crop stage, pest or
symptom observed, approximate number of affected plants or sample units,
distribution in the field, weather, and action taken. If you use traps,
record the trap type, location, and counts. Follow a recognised local
sampling method when available.

============================================================ PART 7.
PLANT DISEASES AND CROP HEALTH
============================================================

Conversation: Understanding leaf symptoms

Farmer: My leaves have yellow spots. What disease is it?

Agricultural Assistant: Yellow spots can have several causes. They may
be linked to a fungal or bacterial disease, a virus, insect feeding,
nutrient deficiency, chemical injury, sun damage, or water stress. I
need more information. Which crop is affected? Are the spots circular or
irregular? Do they have dark edges? Are they on older or younger leaves?
Are stems or fruits also affected? Is the problem spreading?

Farmer: Can I upload a photograph?

Agricultural Assistant: Yes. Take a clear photograph in natural light if
possible. Include one close view of the symptom and another showing the
whole plant. Avoid strong filters. If practical, photograph both the
upper and lower leaf surfaces. Also share the crop name, variety if
known, location, crop age, and recent spray history.

Farmer: Will the AI always identify the disease correctly?

Agricultural Assistant: No. An image model can make mistakes, especially
with poor photographs, unfamiliar varieties, mixed infections, early
symptoms, or diseases not represented in its training data. Treat the
result as a preliminary assessment. A low-confidence result should be
labelled uncertain and referred for expert review when necessary.

Conversation: Preventing disease spread

Farmer: Some plants are infected. Should I remove them?

Agricultural Assistant: It depends on the disease and crop. For some
diseases, removing severely affected plants or plant parts can reduce
sources of infection. For others, removal may not help or may spread
contaminated material if handled incorrectly. Ask a local crop
specialist before taking large-scale action. Clean tools appropriately,
avoid moving contaminated soil or plant material between fields, and
manage crop residues according to local recommendations.

Farmer: Does crop rotation help?

Agricultural Assistant: Crop rotation can reduce some soil-borne pests
and diseases, especially when the next crop is not a suitable host. Its
effectiveness depends on the pathogen’s survival, host range, soil
conditions, and rotation length. It is not a complete solution for every
disease.

Conversation: Treatment tracking

Farmer: I used a recommended treatment. How will I know whether it
worked?

Agricultural Assistant: Record the date, product or method, crop stage,
affected area, and the result. Take photographs from similar angles and
under similar lighting at follow-up. Compare new growth and the spread
of symptoms rather than relying only on old damaged leaves. Some damaged
leaves will not become healthy again even if the treatment is effective.
Look for healthy new growth and whether new symptoms continue to appear.

Farmer: When should I ask an expert?

Agricultural Assistant: Seek expert help when the disease is spreading
rapidly, a large part of the field is affected, the diagnosis is
uncertain, the crop is valuable, treatment has failed, or the symptoms
could be caused by a serious regulated pest or disease. Do not delay
when the problem may affect neighbouring farms.

============================================================ PART 8.
SAFE AND RESPONSIBLE PESTICIDE USE
============================================================

Conversation: Choosing a pesticide

Farmer: Which pesticide should I buy for my crop?

Agricultural Assistant: First identify the pest or disease. A pesticide
must be approved or registered for the relevant crop and target
organism, and the product label must permit the intended use. Do not
select a product only because a shopkeeper or neighbour recommends it.
Check the current label, registration information, crop, target pest,
application rate, protective equipment, waiting period, and
restrictions. A qualified local agricultural officer can help verify the
choice.

Farmer: Can I use a pesticide meant for another crop?

Agricultural Assistant: Do not assume that a product approved for one
crop is safe or permitted for another. Crop safety, residue limits,
application rates, and legal approvals may differ. Use only products
whose current label permits the intended crop and use.

Farmer: Can I mix two pesticides to save time?

Agricultural Assistant: Never assume that two products can be mixed.
Some mixtures may reduce effectiveness, damage the crop, create
hazardous exposure, or cause physical incompatibility. Mix products only
when the labels or a qualified professional explicitly support the
combination. Follow the specified mixing order and precautions where
applicable.

Conversation: Personal protection

Farmer: What should I wear when spraying?

Agricultural Assistant: Follow the product label’s personal protective
equipment instructions. Depending on the product, this may include
suitable gloves, protective clothing, eye protection, footwear, and a
correctly specified respirator. Ordinary cloth masks are not
automatically suitable for chemical vapours or aerosols. Avoid spraying
into the wind and keep children, animals, and unprotected people away
from the application area.

Farmer: Can I eat or smoke while spraying?

Agricultural Assistant: No. Do not eat, drink, or smoke while handling
or applying pesticides. Wash hands and exposed skin after handling.
Remove contaminated clothing carefully and wash it separately according
to the label and local safety guidance. Store products in their original
containers with labels intact.

Farmer: What if pesticide gets on my skin or in my eyes?

Agricultural Assistant: Stop exposure immediately and follow the product
label’s first-aid instructions. Remove contaminated clothing. If the
product contacts the eyes or skin, use the label’s specified rinsing
instructions and seek medical advice promptly. Take the product
container or label information to the healthcare professional if
possible. If someone has breathing difficulty, seizures, collapse, or
severe symptoms, seek emergency medical help immediately. Do not induce
vomiting unless a medical professional or poison service specifically
instructs you to do so.

Conversation: Pre-harvest interval

Farmer: Can I harvest vegetables the day after spraying?

Agricultural Assistant: It depends on the exact product and its label.
The pre-harvest interval is the required time between the last
application and harvesting. It differs by active ingredient,
formulation, crop, and permitted use. Never guess this period. Check the
current product label and follow it exactly. If you do not know what was
sprayed, do not assume the produce is safe to harvest or sell. Seek
advice from an appropriate agricultural or food-safety authority.

============================================================ PART 9.
FRUIT MATURITY, HARVESTING AND QUALITY
============================================================

Conversation: What does ripe mean?

Farmer: My application says a fruit is ripe. Does that mean I should
harvest it immediately?

Agricultural Assistant: Not always. Ripe for eating and mature enough to
harvest are not always the same thing. Some fruits are harvested at
mature-green or other defined maturity stages and ripen during transport
or storage. Other fruits develop their best quality on the plant. The
correct stage depends on the fruit species, variety, intended market,
distance to buyer, storage conditions, and transport time.

Farmer: Can colour tell the maturity?

Agricultural Assistant: Colour can be useful for some fruits, but it is
not enough by itself. Varieties differ, lighting changes how colour
appears, and fruit maturity may also depend on size, firmness, days
after flowering, internal quality, dry matter, or other crop-specific
indicators. A computer vision model should be calibrated for the fruit
and variety.

Farmer: What should the AI result show?

Agricultural Assistant: It should show the fruit type if recognised, the
estimated maturity class, confidence, visible signs, and a clear
explanation that the result is an estimate. If the image is unclear or
the fruit is outside the model’s supported classes, the system should
ask for another image or recommend manual assessment.

Conversation: Harvesting at the right time

Farmer: What happens if I harvest too early?

Agricultural Assistant: Early harvesting may reduce size, flavour,
colour development, weight, and market quality, depending on the crop.
Some fruits may not ripen properly after harvest if picked before the
appropriate physiological maturity stage.

Farmer: What happens if I harvest too late?

Agricultural Assistant: Late harvesting can increase the risk of
softening, cracking, fruit drop, pest damage, disease, over-ripening, or
field losses. The risk depends on crop and weather. Market prices and
labour availability also matter.

Farmer: How should I handle harvested produce?

Agricultural Assistant: Handle produce gently. Avoid throwing or
dropping it. Use clean containers that protect the produce from
crushing. Keep harvested produce out of direct sun when appropriate.
Separate damaged or diseased produce. Use suitable sorting, grading,
ventilation, and packaging for the commodity. Clean tools and
containers. Follow food safety and hygiene requirements.

Conversation: Quality grading

Farmer: Why do buyers ask for grades?

Agricultural Assistant: Grades help buyers understand the quality and
consistency of produce. Depending on the commodity, grading may consider
size, shape, colour, maturity, firmness, visible defects, damage,
cleanliness, and other standards. A grade should be based on an agreed
and transparent method.

Farmer: Can the application grade fruit from a photograph?

Agricultural Assistant: It can estimate visible characteristics if the
model is trained and validated for that commodity. A photograph cannot
reliably measure every internal quality attribute, residue level, hidden
damage, or exact weight. The platform should distinguish visual grading
from laboratory testing or physical inspection.

============================================================ PART 10.
POST-HARVEST STORAGE AND SPOILAGE
============================================================

Conversation: Why does produce spoil?

Farmer: My tomatoes became soft before I could sell them. Why?

Agricultural Assistant: Produce continues to change after harvest.
Respiration, moisture loss, microbial growth, physical injury,
temperature, humidity, and handling conditions affect quality. Some
fruits produce ethylene, a natural plant hormone involved in ripening.
Poor ventilation, unsuitable temperature, rough handling, and delays can
accelerate deterioration.

Farmer: Can I store every fruit in the same cold room?

Agricultural Assistant: No. Different fruits and vegetables have
different temperature and humidity requirements. Some commodities can
suffer chilling injury at temperatures that are too low. Some release
ethylene and can affect nearby produce. Storage should follow
commodity-specific guidance and the facility’s operating instructions.

Farmer: What should I check before booking storage?

Agricultural Assistant: Check the commodity suitability, temperature
range, humidity management, cleanliness, capacity, charges, handling
arrangements, duration, insurance or liability terms, and transport
access. Confirm the actual availability before moving produce.

Conversation: Reducing post-harvest loss

Farmer: I am a small farmer. I cannot afford a cold storage facility.
What can I do?

Agricultural Assistant: You can still reduce losses through better
harvesting, sorting, shade, clean crates, careful handling, timely sale,
and suitable short-term storage. You can also explore shared storage
through farmer producer organisations, cooperatives, collection centres,
packhouses, or local service providers. The right option depends on the
crop and local infrastructure.

Farmer: Should I wash produce before selling?

Agricultural Assistant: It depends on the crop, buyer specification,
available clean water, drying facilities, and packaging requirements.
Washing with contaminated water can create food safety problems. Wet
produce packed without proper drying may spoil faster. Follow
commodity-specific handling standards and buyer requirements.

============================================================ PART 11.
MARKET ACCESS, BUYERS AND SELLING
============================================================

Conversation: How should I decide where to sell?

Farmer: A buyer offered me a price. How do I know whether it is fair?

Agricultural Assistant: Compare the offer with recent prices for the
same commodity, variety, quality, quantity, and market location. Also
consider transport, loading, weighing, packaging, commission, payment
timing, rejection conditions, and other deductions. A higher quoted
price may not mean a higher final amount received.

Farmer: What is the difference between a quoted price and the amount I
receive?

Agricultural Assistant: The final amount may change after weighing,
quality grading, transport charges, market fees, commissions, and agreed
deductions. Ask for the full terms in writing or in a message you can
keep. Make sure the quantity, grade, price per unit, delivery location,
and payment date are clear.

Farmer: How can the application help me find buyers?

Agricultural Assistant: It can create a listing with crop, variety,
quantity, quality, location, expected availability, photographs, and
price expectations. Buyers can search and send offers. You can compare
offers and select one. The application should show buyer verification
status and transaction history where available.

Conversation: Group selling

Farmer: I only have 100 kilograms, but the buyer wants 500 kilograms.
What can I do?

Agricultural Assistant: You may be able to join a collective sale with
nearby farmers who have the same crop and acceptable quality. The
platform can combine quantities while keeping each farmer’s contribution
and payment separate.

Farmer: What should we agree before joining?

Agricultural Assistant: Agree on crop variety, quality standard,
quantity, weighing method, price, collection date, transport cost,
packaging, rejection rules, and payment allocation. Each farmer should
know how much produce is included and how much money they will receive.
Do not combine produce of very different quality without a transparent
grading arrangement.

Conversation: Buyer verification and fraud prevention

Farmer: Someone says they will buy my produce but asks me to pay a
registration fee first. Is that safe?

Agricultural Assistant: Be careful. Verify the buyer’s identity and
business details through reliable channels. Do not pay unexplained
advance fees or share OTPs, passwords, or banking PINs. Use documented
order terms and traceable payment methods. If the platform provides a
verified-buyer badge, understand what was actually checked; verification
is not a guarantee that every future transaction is risk-free.

Farmer: What if the buyer refuses to pay?

Agricultural Assistant: Keep the order confirmation, agreed terms,
weighment records, delivery proof, messages, and payment details. Use
the platform’s dispute process and seek help from the relevant market
authority, farmer organisation, or legal support where appropriate. The
platform should not promise recovery unless it has a real and lawful
mechanism to do so.

Conversation: Understanding mandi prices

Farmer: What is the modal price?

Agricultural Assistant: In market reporting, the modal price is
generally the price at which the largest volume of transactions occurred
during the reporting period. Minimum and maximum prices show the lower
and upper reported prices. These figures can vary by market, quality,
variety, and date. Always check the reporting time and unit.

Farmer: Can the application tell me tomorrow’s exact price?

Agricultural Assistant: No system can guarantee tomorrow’s exact market
price. A model may estimate trends using historical prices, arrivals,
seasonality, weather, and other factors, but the estimate can be wrong.
Show forecasts as uncertain scenarios, not guaranteed prices.

============================================================ PART 12.
FARM EXPENSES, PROFIT AND FINANCIAL PLANNING
============================================================

Conversation: Calculating crop profit

Farmer: I sold my crop for a good amount, but I still do not know
whether I made a profit.

Agricultural Assistant: To calculate profit, record all costs associated
with the crop. Include seed, fertilizer, crop protection, labour,
irrigation, machinery, land preparation, harvesting, packaging,
transport, storage, market charges, and other expenses. Then compare the
total cost with the money actually received from sales.

Farmer: Should I include family labour?

Agricultural Assistant: For a complete economic picture, it is useful to
estimate the value of family labour even if no cash payment was made.
You can maintain both cash expenses and estimated economic costs. This
helps you understand the real cost of production.

Farmer: What is break-even price?

Agricultural Assistant: Break-even price is the approximate selling
price per unit needed to cover the total cost, assuming the saleable
quantity is known. If the crop has multiple grades or some produce is
lost, calculate using the quantity actually sold or expected to be sold.
Make sure all costs use consistent units.

Farmer: Can the application tell me which crop will earn the most?

Agricultural Assistant: It can compare scenarios using estimated input
costs, expected yield, historical or current market information, water
needs, crop duration, and risk. But it should not promise a profit.
Prices, weather, pests, labour, and yield can change. Show assumptions
clearly and encourage farmers to consider local suitability and risk.

Conversation: Keeping farm accounts

Farmer: I find it difficult to maintain accounts every day.

Agricultural Assistant: Start with a simple record. You can record an
expense by speaking it, selecting a category, or asking a village
assistant to enter it. For example: “I spent 800 rupees on labour
today.” The application can save the date, amount, category, crop, and
description. Review the records weekly or after each major activity.

============================================================ PART 13.
SEEDS, VARIETIES AND CROP PLANNING
============================================================

Conversation: Selecting seeds

Farmer: Which seed variety should I buy?

Agricultural Assistant: The suitable variety depends on your location,
season, soil, irrigation, crop duration, market preference, disease
pressure, and local recommendations. Check whether the variety is
suitable for your agro-climatic region and intended market. Purchase
from a reliable and authorised source. Check the seed label, lot number,
germination information, expiry or validity details where applicable,
and any certification information.

Farmer: Is the most expensive seed always the best?

Agricultural Assistant: No. Price alone does not prove suitability or
quality. Compare the variety’s characteristics, local performance, seed
quality information, seller reliability, and suitability for your farm.
Be cautious of unrealistic yield claims.

Conversation: Seed storage

Farmer: Can I keep leftover seeds for the next season?

Agricultural Assistant: Storage life depends on crop species, seed
moisture, packaging, temperature, humidity, and initial seed quality.
Keep seeds labelled and stored in a clean, dry, cool place according to
the supplier’s instructions. Some seeds lose viability quickly. Before
sowing saved seed, check its condition and germination where
appropriate. Do not assume all leftover seed will remain suitable.

Conversation: Crop rotation and diversification

Farmer: Why do agricultural officers recommend crop rotation?

Agricultural Assistant: Growing different crops in sequence can help
break pest and disease cycles, improve nutrient use, diversify income,
and reduce dependence on one crop. The benefits depend on the crops
selected and local conditions. Legumes can contribute biologically fixed
nitrogen through suitable root bacteria, but the amount available to the
next crop depends on biomass, residue management, and other factors.
Rotation should be planned for local markets, water availability, and
soil needs.

============================================================ PART 14.
POLLINATION, FLOWERING AND FRUIT SET
============================================================

Conversation: Flowers are falling

Farmer: My crop is flowering, but many flowers are falling. Why?

Agricultural Assistant: Flower drop can occur because of heat, cold,
irregular watering, excessive vegetative growth, nutrient imbalance,
poor pollination, pest damage, disease, or natural crop behaviour. The
cause differs by crop. Tell me the crop, growth stage, recent
temperature, irrigation pattern, and whether insects are visiting the
flowers.

Farmer: Can insects help my crop?

Agricultural Assistant: Yes. Bees and other pollinators support
pollination in many crops. Protect pollinators by avoiding unnecessary
pesticide applications during flowering and following label
restrictions. Where pesticide use is required, seek advice on
pollinator-safe timing and methods. Do not spray flowering crops in a
way that exposes bees or other beneficial insects to harmful chemicals.

Farmer: Why are fruits forming but remaining small?

Agricultural Assistant: Small fruit can be related to variety, poor
pollination, water stress, nutrient imbalance, excessive fruit load,
pests, disease, or environmental stress. Check whether the crop has
enough suitable water and whether the problem affects all fruits or only
certain branches or plants. A local specialist can help distinguish the
cause.

============================================================ PART 15.
WEEDS AND FIELD MANAGEMENT
============================================================

Conversation: Why should I control weeds?

Farmer: There are many weeds in my field. Are they really harmful?

Agricultural Assistant: Weeds can compete with crops for light, water,
nutrients, and space. Some weeds host pests or diseases and can
interfere with harvesting. However, not every plant growing between crop
rows is automatically harmful. Management depends on the crop, weed
species, density, and growth stage.

Farmer: What are the ways to manage weeds?

Agricultural Assistant: Methods include clean seed, crop rotation,
timely land preparation, suitable spacing, mulching, hand weeding,
mechanical cultivation, cover crops, and approved herbicides when
necessary. Integrated weed management combines suitable methods over
time.

Farmer: Can I use any herbicide on any crop?

Agricultural Assistant: No. Herbicide selectivity and permitted use
differ. A product may damage the crop if applied incorrectly or to the
wrong crop stage. Follow the current label and local agricultural
advice. Avoid drift onto neighbouring crops, water bodies, and sensitive
areas.

============================================================ PART 16.
LIVESTOCK AND MIXED FARMING
============================================================

Conversation: Integrating crops and livestock

Farmer: I keep cattle along with farming. Can the application help with
both?

Agricultural Assistant: A broader farm management platform can record
livestock information as well as crop information. It may include animal
identification, vaccination reminders, feed records, breeding records,
milk production, and veterinary contacts. However, animal health
diagnosis and treatment should be handled by qualified veterinary
professionals.

Farmer: Can I use animal manure in my field?

Agricultural Assistant: Properly managed manure can contribute organic
matter and nutrients. Its nutrient content varies with animal diet,
bedding, storage, and handling. Poorly managed manure can carry
pathogens, create odour, contaminate water, or cause nutrient losses.
Composting or other approved treatment and safe application timing are
important. Follow food safety and local manure-management guidance.

Farmer: My animal is sick. Can the crop AI diagnose it?

Agricultural Assistant: A plant disease model should not be used to
diagnose animals. Livestock health requires a separate veterinary
workflow and suitable expertise. If an animal is seriously ill, unable
to stand, having difficulty breathing, bleeding, or showing severe
symptoms, contact a veterinarian promptly.

============================================================ PART 17.
FARMER ORGANISATIONS AND SHARED SERVICES
============================================================

Conversation: What is an FPO?

Farmer: I have heard people talk about an FPO. What does it mean?

Agricultural Assistant: A Farmer Producer Organisation is a collective
organisation formed by farmers to improve their ability to access
inputs, services, markets, processing, storage, and other opportunities.
The legal structure and services vary. Farmers should understand
membership conditions, governance, fees, responsibilities, and
benefit-sharing arrangements before joining.

Farmer: How can a digital agriculture platform support an FPO?

Agricultural Assistant: It can help an FPO register member farmers,
collect crop and expected harvest information, aggregate produce,
coordinate quality grading, organise transport, negotiate with buyers,
manage input orders, and maintain transaction records. It can also help
members access expert advice and shared equipment.

Conversation: Sharing equipment

Farmer: I need a tractor only for one day. Buying one is too expensive.

Agricultural Assistant: A verified equipment rental marketplace can help
you find nearby tractor owners or service providers. Check the hourly or
daily rate, operator charges, fuel, transport, deposit, booking time,
cancellation terms, and responsibility for damage. Confirm the equipment
is suitable and safe for the intended work.

Farmer: What should the vendor dashboard include?

Agricultural Assistant: Vendors should be able to register equipment,
provide photos and specifications, set availability, publish transparent
rates, accept or reject booking requests, record completed jobs, and
manage customer complaints. Farmers should be able to see verification
status and booking terms before confirming.

============================================================ PART 18.
FARM INSURANCE, DISASTER SUPPORT AND GOVERNMENT SERVICES
============================================================

Conversation: Crop insurance

Farmer: Heavy rain damaged my crop. Can I get compensation?

Agricultural Assistant: Eligibility depends on the insurance policy,
notified crop and area, enrolment status, cause of loss, reporting
deadline, and applicable scheme rules. Keep your policy details, crop
records, photographs, date of damage, and other required documents.
Contact the official insurance channel or local agriculture department
promptly. Do not rely on an unofficial message about eligibility.

Farmer: Can the application file my claim automatically?

Agricultural Assistant: It may help organise documents, explain the
process, create reminders, and guide you to official claim channels.
Actual submission requires the permitted integration, farmer consent,
correct documents, and compliance with the scheme’s current process.
Never claim that a submission is complete unless the official system
confirms it.

Conversation: Government agricultural schemes

Farmer: Which government scheme can help me?

Agricultural Assistant: It depends on your state, landholding or tenancy
situation, crop, income category where relevant, social eligibility
criteria, and the purpose of support. Schemes change over time. The
assistant should ask your state and the type of help you need, then
retrieve current information from official government sources.

Farmer: Someone sent me a link asking for my bank OTP to receive a
subsidy.

Agricultural Assistant: Do not share an OTP, PIN, password, or full
banking credentials with anyone. Verify the scheme through its official
government website, local government office, bank, or authorised service
centre. Be careful of fake registration links and requests for
unexplained fees.

Conversation: Natural disasters

Farmer: My field was flooded. What should I do first?

Agricultural Assistant: First protect people and animals. Do not enter
floodwater if there may be electrical hazards, unstable ground, or
dangerous currents. Follow local emergency instructions. Once it is
safe, document the damage, note the affected area, and contact the local
agriculture department or insurance provider as applicable. Avoid eating
or selling produce that may be contaminated until it has been assessed
under relevant food safety guidance.

============================================================ PART 19.
DIGITAL AGRICULTURE FOR FARMERS WITH LIMITED SMARTPHONE ACCESS
============================================================

Conversation: I do not know how to use an application

Farmer: I have a basic mobile phone. I cannot use a complicated
application.

Agricultural Assistant: You should still be able to access important
agricultural information. A platform can provide a telephone-based
service, voice menu, SMS alerts, and assistance through a village
agricultural assistant or a nearby service centre. You should not have
to learn a complex smartphone interface just to ask about your crop.

Farmer: How does a missed-call service work?

Agricultural Assistant: You call a designated number and disconnect. The
platform recognises the registered number and may call you back. A voice
menu can guide you through options using the phone keypad. The exact
service depends on the telephony provider and whether your number has
been registered with consent.

Farmer: Can the system speak in my language?

Agricultural Assistant: It should support selected regional languages
where reliable speech recognition and voice output are available. The
platform should allow you to choose your preferred language. If
automatic language recognition is uncertain, it should ask you to select
the language rather than guessing.

Conversation: Voice messages

Farmer: I cannot type a long question. Can I speak?

Agricultural Assistant: Yes, on a supported smartphone or through a
telephone service. You can describe the problem in your own words. The
system should convert speech to text, identify the crop and concern, and
ask a short follow-up question if needed. It should read the answer
aloud when possible.

Farmer: What if the system misunderstands me?

Agricultural Assistant: It should repeat the important details and ask
you to confirm them. For example: “I heard that your chilli leaves are
curling. Is that correct?” You should be able to correct the crop,
symptom, or location.

Conversation: Village agricultural assistant

Farmer: Can someone help me use the service?

Agricultural Assistant: A trained village agricultural assistant can
help you register, record crop details, take photographs, create a
disease case, find buyers, and access agricultural services. You should
be told what information is being entered and be able to review
important details. The assistant should not make unauthorised decisions
or control your money.

Farmer: What if the assistant enters the wrong crop or quantity?

Agricultural Assistant: The system should allow corrections and maintain
a record of important changes. Before a listing or order is confirmed,
the farmer should hear or see the crop, quantity, price, and other key
terms in a language they understand.

============================================================ PART 20.
HOW A RAG AGRICULTURAL ASSISTANT SHOULD RESPOND
============================================================

Conversation: A farmer asks a vague question

Farmer: My crop is dying. Tell me a medicine.

Good assistant response: “I am sorry your crop is having trouble. I do
not want to suggest the wrong medicine. Which crop is it, and what
changes are you seeing? Are the leaves yellow, spotted, curled, or dry?
Is the problem affecting a few plants or most of the field? If you can,
share a clear photograph of the whole plant and one close photograph of
the affected part.”

Why this is good: It avoids guessing, asks relevant questions, and seeks
evidence before recommending treatment.

Poor assistant response: “Spray pesticide X immediately.”

Why this is poor: It assumes a diagnosis, may cause crop damage, can
expose people and the environment to unnecessary chemicals, and may
recommend an unapproved use.

Conversation: The farmer asks for a fertilizer dose

Farmer: How much urea should I apply to one acre?

Good assistant response: “The correct amount depends on your crop, crop
stage, soil test, previous fertilizer applications, and local
recommendation. Which crop are you growing, and how old is it? If you
have a soil test or nutrient recommendation, share it. I can help you
understand the information, but I should not guess a fertilizer dose
without the necessary details.”

Why this is good: It recognises that fertilizer rates are crop- and
context-specific.

Conversation: The farmer asks about a current price

Farmer: What is today’s tomato price in my market?

Good assistant response: “I can help you compare prices, but today’s
price needs live market information. Which market or district do you
want to check? I will also need to distinguish the reported market price
from a confirmed buyer offer. Please check the date, unit, quality and
market before making a selling decision.”

Why this is good: The corpus cannot provide today’s live price. The RAG
system must use an up-to-date market source.

Conversation: The farmer asks about a disease from an image

Farmer: This leaf has brown spots. What is the disease?

Good assistant response: “Brown spots can be caused by different
diseases or by non-disease problems. Please tell me the crop, location
and crop age. A clear photograph of the upper and lower leaf surfaces
and the whole plant will help. I can provide a preliminary assessment,
but a photograph alone may not confirm the disease.”

Why this is good: It distinguishes a visual symptom from a confirmed
diagnosis.

Conversation: The farmer asks whether a fruit is ready

Farmer: My mango looks yellow. Can I harvest it?

Good assistant response: “Colour is one clue, but mango maturity varies
by variety and growing conditions. What variety is it, and is the fruit
intended for local sale or long-distance transport? The correct harvest
stage may differ. A maturity assessment should consider more than colour
alone.”

Why this is good: It avoids treating colour as a universal maturity
indicator.

============================================================ PART 22.
AGRICULTURAL GLOSSARY IN SIMPLE LANGUAGE
============================================================

Agriculture: The practice of growing crops, raising livestock, and
producing food and other farm products.

Agro-climatic zone: A region with broadly similar climate, soil, and
agricultural conditions.

Germination: The process by which a seed begins to grow.

Seedling: A young plant that has developed from a seed.

Transplanting: Moving young plants from a nursery or one location to the
field.

Vegetative growth: The stage when a plant develops roots, stems, and
leaves.

Flowering: The stage when a plant produces flowers.

Pollination: Transfer of pollen that enables fertilisation in flowering
plants.

Fruit set: The stage when a flower develops into a young fruit.

Maturity: A stage when a crop or fruit has developed sufficiently for
its intended use or harvest.

Harvesting: Collecting the mature crop or produce.

Yield: The quantity of crop produced from a given area.

Crop rotation: Growing different crops in a planned sequence on the same
land.

Intercropping: Growing two or more crops together in a planned
arrangement.

Mulching: Covering the soil surface with suitable organic or other
materials to manage moisture, weeds, and soil conditions.

Compost: Decomposed organic material used to improve soil.

Farmyard manure: Organic manure made from livestock dung, urine, and
bedding material after suitable handling and decomposition.

Soil pH: A measure of how acidic or alkaline soil is.

Salinity: The presence of excessive soluble salts in soil or water.

Soil organic carbon: Carbon contained in soil organic matter, used as an
indicator of soil condition.

Nitrogen: A major plant nutrient important for leaf and stem growth.

Phosphorus: A major plant nutrient involved in energy transfer, roots,
and reproductive development.

Potassium: A major plant nutrient involved in water regulation and many
plant processes.

Micronutrients: Essential plant nutrients required in small quantities.

Fertigation: Applying suitable soluble nutrients through an irrigation
system.

Drip irrigation: Applying water slowly near plant roots through
emitters.

Waterlogging: A condition where excess water fills soil pores and
reduces oxygen around roots.

Evapotranspiration: Water loss through evaporation from soil and
transpiration from plants.

Pest: An organism that causes harm to crops or stored produce.

Pathogen: An organism or infectious agent that causes disease.

Fungus: A group of organisms that includes many plant disease-causing
species as well as beneficial species.

Bacteria: Microscopic organisms; some cause plant diseases and others
are beneficial.

Virus: An infectious agent that can cause plant disease and often
depends on living host cells to multiply.

Integrated Pest Management: A strategy that combines monitoring and
multiple management methods to control pests responsibly.

Biological control: Using natural enemies or biological agents to reduce
pests or diseases.

Pre-harvest interval: The required time between the last pesticide
application and harvest, as specified by the product label.

Post-harvest loss: Reduction in quantity or quality after harvesting.

Grading: Sorting produce according to agreed quality characteristics.

Cold chain: Temperature-managed handling and transport from production
to storage, processing, or sale.

Mandi: An agricultural wholesale market.

Modal price: The price at which the largest volume of reported
transactions occurred in a market during a reporting period.

FPO: Farmer Producer Organisation, a collective organisation formed by
farmers.

IVR: Interactive Voice Response, a telephone system that lets callers
use voice or keypad choices to access information.

RAG: Retrieval-Augmented Generation, a method in which an AI system
retrieves relevant information from a knowledge collection before
generating an answer.

============================================================ PART 23.
RULES FOR THE AGRICULTURAL RAG SYSTEM
============================================================

1.  Give priority to retrieved evidence relevant to the farmer’s crop,
    location, growth stage, and question.
2.  Do not treat general agricultural information as a crop-specific
    prescription.
3.  Ask for missing information when it changes the safety or
    correctness of an answer.
4.  Use simple language and explain technical words.
5.  Prefer short, practical steps for farmers who use voice or basic
    phones.
6.  Provide more detailed explanations when the farmer asks for them.
7.  Distinguish confirmed information, estimates, model predictions, and
    assumptions.
8.  Never invent live weather, market prices, stock availability, scheme
    rules, or government approvals.
9.  Use official and current sources for changing regulatory or scheme
    information.
10. Never invent pesticide rates, mixing instructions, or pre-harvest
    intervals.
11. Never recommend a pesticide solely from an uncertain image
    classification.
12. Encourage expert confirmation for severe, spreading, unusual, or
    uncertain crop problems.
13. Do not claim that a crop is safe for consumption based only on an
    image.
14. Do not claim that a buyer, seller, or vendor is verified unless the
    platform’s verification record supports it.
15. Protect personal and farm location information.
16. Never ask for OTPs, passwords, PINs, or unnecessary financial
    credentials.
17. If the farmer cannot read, offer a concise spoken explanation or
    assisted service.
18. If the farmer has no smartphone, offer the available telephone, SMS,
    or village-assistant channel.
19. Explain uncertainty honestly.
20. When evidence is missing, say what needs to be checked rather than
    inventing an answer.

============================================================ PART 24.
SAMPLE EVERYDAY FARMER CONVERSATIONS
============================================================

Conversation: Yellow leaves in tomato

Farmer: My tomato leaves are turning yellow. Is it because of less
fertilizer?

Assistant: It could be related to nutrients, but yellow leaves can also
occur because of overwatering, root damage, disease, pests, or natural
ageing. Are the older leaves yellowing first, or are the young leaves
yellowing? Is the whole leaf yellow, or are the veins remaining green?
Is the soil wet or dry?

Farmer: The older leaves are yellow and the soil is wet.

Assistant: Wet soil may be reducing oxygen around the roots, especially
if drainage is poor. Check whether water is standing in the field and
whether the roots look healthy. Avoid adding fertilizer immediately
without checking the cause. If the soil remains waterlogged, improve
drainage where feasible and seek local advice if many plants are
affected.

Conversation: Chilli leaves curling

Farmer: The leaves of my chilli plants are curling.

Assistant: Leaf curling can be caused by sucking insects, viruses, heat,
water stress, or chemical injury. Check the underside of the leaves and
growing points for insects, eggs, or sticky deposits. Are the leaves
also yellow or distorted? Is the problem spreading from one area? A
clear photograph can help with preliminary assessment.

Farmer: I see tiny insects below the leaves.

Assistant: That is useful information. Please take a clear photograph of
the insects and affected leaves. Correct pest identification matters
because different insects require different management. Avoid spraying
an unidentified chemical. You can ask a local agricultural specialist to
confirm the pest and suitable IPM options.

Conversation: Paddy field water management

Farmer: Should I keep water standing in my paddy field all the time?

Assistant: Water management in rice depends on the establishment method,
soil, variety, crop stage, local recommendations, and irrigation system.
Continuous standing water is not necessary in every rice-growing system.
Some water-saving methods use controlled irrigation and periods without
standing water, but they must be managed carefully. Follow local
agricultural guidance and ensure the crop does not experience harmful
water stress.

Conversation: Mango fruit falling

Farmer: Small mango fruits are falling from my tree. Is this a disease?

Assistant: Some fruit drop is natural, but excessive drop may be related
to weather stress, poor pollination, pests, disease, nutrient or water
problems, or tree condition. When did the fruit drop begin? Are the
fallen fruits clean, damaged, or showing spots? Has there been strong
wind, heat, or irregular watering? These details can help narrow the
possibilities.

Conversation: Banana leaves tearing

Farmer: My banana leaves are splitting. Is the plant sick?

Assistant: Banana leaves can split due to wind and are not always a sign
of disease. Check whether the leaf tissue is otherwise green and
healthy. Look for unusual spots, yellowing, wilting, or damage to the
pseudostem. If the plant is otherwise healthy, wind-related tearing may
be the main explanation. Severe or unusual symptoms should be inspected
locally.

Conversation: Groundnut yellowing

Farmer: My groundnut crop looks pale. What should I check?

Assistant: Check the crop stage, field moisture, drainage, root health,
nodules, pest pressure, and soil test history. Legumes such as groundnut
can obtain nitrogen through biological nitrogen fixation when suitable
root-nodule bacteria are present and conditions are favourable. But poor
nodulation, nutrient deficiencies, waterlogging, and other stresses can
affect growth. A soil or plant assessment can help identify the actual
issue.

Conversation: Onion storage

Farmer: Can I store freshly harvested onions in a closed room?

Assistant: Onions need appropriate curing and storage conditions.
Storing wet or poorly cured bulbs in a closed, humid space can encourage
decay. Follow local recommendations for curing, ventilation, sorting,
and storage. Remove damaged or diseased bulbs and inspect stored produce
regularly. Storage conditions should match the variety and local
climate.

Conversation: Farmer wants to sell quickly

Farmer: I have 300 kg of vegetables and they may spoil soon. What should
I do?

Assistant: First tell me the vegetable, approximate harvest date,
current condition, and your location. We should prioritise a quick and
realistic sale. Check nearby buyers, local markets, collection centres,
and suitable storage. Compare the actual net amount after transport and
other charges. Separate damaged produce from sound produce and do not
misrepresent quality.

Conversation: Farmer cannot access the internet

Farmer: My internet is not working. Can I still get help?

Assistant: If the service supports telephone access, you can use its IVR
or callback number. You may also receive selected alerts by SMS. If you
have access to a village agricultural assistant or service centre, they
can help submit your request. Live market prices, current weather and
new AI image analysis may need connectivity, but saved information may
remain available offline.

source, publication date, jurisdiction, crop applicability, and review
date as metadata wherever possible. Review safety-critical agricultural
content with qualified agronomists before deploying it to farmers.

IMPORTANT KNOWLEDGE-BASE RULE
Government schemes, subsidy budgets, eligibility rules, application windows, crop lists,
premium rates, district targets and office contacts can change. The information below is
a navigation and education layer, not a guarantee that an application is open or that a
farmer will receive a particular amount. Before giving a definitive answer, retrieve the
latest official notification for the farmer's state, district, taluk, crop, category and
financial year. Never invent a subsidy percentage, rupee amount, deadline, beneficiary
quota or approval. If current details are unavailable, say so and direct the farmer to
the relevant department or official portal.

A farmer should never pay an agent a bribe or share an OTP, UPI PIN, ATM PIN, bank
password or full card details to obtain a government benefit. Official scheme application
may involve a legitimate service fee at an authorised centre; ask for a printed receipt.

SECTION 1 — HOW A FARMER SHOULD FIND AND APPLY FOR A GOVERNMENT BENEFIT

A farmer may say:
"Government has announced a subsidy. How do I know whether I can get it?"

A helpful answer:
"First, tell me your state, district, taluk and village; what crop or activity you do;
whether you own, lease or share-cultivate the land; your land area; and what support you
need. A scheme may be available only for selected crops, districts, categories or a
limited number of beneficiaries. We should check the current official notification before
you spend money or buy equipment."

Recommended application journey:
1. Identify the farmer's actual need: income support, seed, fertilizer, irrigation,
   machinery, horticulture, livestock, storage, crop insurance, marketing or a loan.
2. Identify the responsible department. Agriculture, Horticulture, Animal Husbandry,
   Fisheries, Sericulture, Cooperation, Rural Development and banks administer different
   benefits.
3. Confirm the farmer's state, district, taluk, hobli and village. Do not assume that a
   scheme available in one district is available in another.
4. Check the current financial-year or crop-season notification and application window.
5. Check eligibility, land-record requirements, beneficiary category, crop notification,
   minimum/maximum area, approved equipment list, vendor conditions and subsidy ceiling.
6. Ask whether the farmer must obtain approval before purchasing an input or machine.
   Many capital subsidies require prior approval, inspection or purchase from an approved
   vendor. Do not tell a farmer to buy first unless the official rules explicitly permit it.
7. Prepare the documents and apply through the official portal, department office,
   Raitha Samparka Kendra, bank, authorised Common Service Centre (CSC) or other
   designated channel.
8. Obtain an acknowledgement/application number and keep a copy.
9. Track the application and respond to any inspection or document request.
10. Verify the DBT credit in the farmer's own bank account. Do not treat an application
    submission as approval or a sanction as proof that money has been credited.
11. If rejected or delayed, ask for the written reason and use the official grievance
    channel.

Common documents that may be requested (requirements differ by scheme):
- Aadhaar or another accepted identity document.
- Active mobile number; Aadhaar-linked mobile may be needed for OTP-based e-KYC.
- Bank passbook or account details in the beneficiary's own name, as specified.
- Land record / Record of Rights (RTC or Pahani in Karnataka), mutation record, survey
  number and land area.
- Farmer registration / FRUITS ID in Karnataka where required.
- Recent photograph.
- Caste certificate for category-specific benefits, where required.
- Income certificate, where required.
- Crop details, season, sowing details and notified crop information.
- Lease, tenancy, consent or cultivation proof if accepted under the particular scheme.
- Quotation, invoice, equipment specification or approved-vendor details for asset
  subsidies.
- Soil test report, water source details or project report where relevant.
- Previous insurance policy, bank loan or application details where relevant.

Never assume every scheme requires all these documents. Tell the farmer exactly which
documents the current scheme notification asks for. Never ask the farmer to post Aadhaar,
bank account numbers, OTPs or identity documents in a public chat.

SECTION 2 — CENTRAL GOVERNMENT AGRICULTURE SCHEMES

2.1 PM-KISAN — Pradhan Mantri Kisan Samman Nidhi
Purpose:
Income support for eligible landholding farmer families through direct benefit transfer.

Core structure:
- The scheme provides Rs. 6,000 per eligible farmer family per year in three equal
  instalments, subject to the scheme's current guidelines and eligibility checks.
- The benefit is transferred to the eligible beneficiary's bank account through DBT.
- The definition of farmer family and exclusion categories are set by the official
  guidelines. It is not correct to say that every person who works on a farm qualifies.
- Institutional landholders and specified higher-income or public-office/professional
  categories are among the exclusions described in the official guidelines.
- e-KYC is mandatory for registered beneficiaries. The official portal provides status
  checking and registration services.

How to help a farmer:
1. Check whether the farmer's landholding and family details meet the current rules.
2. Check PM-KISAN beneficiary status and any pending e-KYC or land-seeding issue.
3. Confirm Aadhaar/bank account validation and the correct beneficiary details.
4. Use the official portal or visit an authorised CSC / agriculture office for biometric
   assistance if the farmer cannot complete digital e-KYC.
5. Keep the acknowledgement and check the official "Know Your Status" facility.

Official portal: https://pmkisan.gov.in/
Official e-KYC information: https://pmkisan.gov.in/KnowAboutEKYC.aspx

RAG response example:
Farmer: "I have two acres. Can I get PM-KISAN?"
Assistant: "Land area alone does not confirm eligibility. PM-KISAN checks the eligible
farmer-family and land-record details, along with exclusion rules. Which state and
district is your land in? If you are in Karnataka, your Raitha Samparka Kendra or an
authorised CSC can help check your FRUITS/land records and PM-KISAN status. Please do
not share your Aadhaar number or OTP here."

2.2 PMFBY — Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana
Purpose:
Crop insurance for notified crops and areas against specified crop-loss risks, subject to
the policy and operational guidelines for that season.

Key points:
- Availability depends on the state, district, notified crop, season and insurance
  notification. Not every crop in every village is automatically covered.
- Farmer premium contribution under the published PMFBY framework is capped by crop
  category and season. The official FAQ describes maximum farmer-paid rates of 2% of
  sum insured for Kharif foodgrain/oilseed crops, 1.5% for Rabi foodgrain/oilseed crops,
  and 5% for annual commercial/horticultural crops, or the actuarial rate if lower.
  The current season notification must still be checked.
- Enrolment cut-off dates are seasonal and can differ by state and crop.
- A farmer should report a localised calamity or post-harvest loss within the time and
  through the method prescribed in the current policy. Do not assume a universal deadline.
- Keep the application receipt, policy number, bank details, crop and sowing information.
- The farmer should not wait until the season ends to ask whether a crop is insured.

How to help after crop damage:
1. Ask the farmer's village, crop, season and date of damage.
2. Ask whether the crop and area were notified and whether a policy was purchased.
3. Tell the farmer to report loss immediately using the official channel and within the
   current policy's reporting window.
4. Preserve photographs, field records, policy details and acknowledgement.
5. Do not promise compensation. Assessment and payment depend on the policy, notification,
   loss assessment and claim process.

Official portal: https://pmfby.gov.in/
Official helpline shown on the portal: 14447 (Krishi Rakshak Portal & Helpline).
The farmer should verify current availability and reporting instructions on the portal.

2.3 Kisan Credit Card (KCC) and Modified Interest Subvention Scheme
Purpose:
Short-term institutional credit for eligible farmers' crop cultivation and certain allied
activities, subject to bank appraisal and current government/banking rules.

What it can help finance:
- Crop cultivation expenses and eligible working capital.
- Certain post-harvest and allied activity needs where permitted by the current KCC rules.
- Dairy, fisheries and animal husbandry working capital may have separate KCC pathways.

How to guide:
- Approach a bank, Regional Rural Bank, cooperative bank or eligible financial institution.
- Ask for the KCC application checklist, scale of finance for the crop and district, interest
  rate, collateral rules, repayment schedule and current interest-subvention conditions.
- A subsidy or interest benefit is not automatic; it depends on eligibility, prompt repayment
  conditions and current government/bank rules.
- Do not encourage a farmer to borrow more than the realistic seasonal requirement.
- Explain the difference between a loan, an interest concession and a grant. A loan must
  normally be repaid.

2.4 Pradhan Mantri Krishi Sinchayee Yojana (PMKSY) — Per Drop More Crop
Purpose:
Improve water-use efficiency and support micro-irrigation such as drip and sprinkler
systems, as implemented through the relevant state department and current guidelines.

Potential benefits:
- Support for eligible drip and sprinkler irrigation systems.
- Water-use efficiency and improved irrigation management.
- The subsidy rate, eligible area, crop, farmer category, equipment specification and
  approved supplier conditions must be checked in the current state notification.
- Historical district pages may show high subsidy rates, but old percentages must not be
  presented as guaranteed current rates.

Before purchase:
Ask the farmer to obtain written confirmation of eligibility, approved specifications,
approved vendor and whether pre-sanction inspection is required. Keep the quotation and
application acknowledgement.

2.5 Agriculture Infrastructure Fund (AIF)
Purpose:
Financing support for eligible post-harvest management infrastructure and community
farming assets through eligible lending institutions and project structures.

Examples of eligible project types may include:
- Warehouses and silos.
- Pack houses, sorting and grading facilities.
- Cold-chain and cold-storage infrastructure.
- Primary processing and eligible supply-chain infrastructure.
- Community farming assets covered by current guidelines.

Important:
AIF is primarily a financing facility with defined interest-subvention and credit-guarantee
features under its guidelines; it should not be described as a cash grant automatically
paid to every farmer. Eligibility, project type, loan amount, lender and current scheme
window must be verified.

Official portal: https://agriinfra.dac.gov.in/

2.6 Sub-Mission on Agricultural Mechanization (SMAM)
Purpose:
Promote access to farm machinery and mechanisation, including support for eligible
individuals, groups, cooperatives, FPOs and Custom Hiring Centres, depending on current
guidelines.

Possible equipment categories:
- Tractors and power tillers where eligible.
- Seed drills, planters and transplanters.
- Harvesters and threshers.
- Sprayers and plant-protection equipment.
- Residue-management machinery.
- Equipment for small and marginal farms and custom-hiring facilities.

Rules:
- Subsidy percentages and maximum amounts vary by machine, beneficiary category, state
  allocation and current notification.
- A machine may need to be on the approved list.
- Some schemes require online application and prior approval.
- Never recommend purchasing machinery before checking the current approval process.

2.7 Soil Health Card and Soil Health & Fertility support
Purpose:
Promote soil testing and balanced nutrient management.

Farmer-facing value:
- Soil sample testing.
- Information on soil pH, organic carbon and nutrient status.
- Crop-specific fertilizer recommendations based on test results.
- Better nutrient management and reduced unnecessary fertilizer application.

A Soil Health Card is not a promise of free fertilizer. The farmer should use the test
recommendation and ask an agriculture officer how to correct the identified deficiency.

Official information: https://soilhealth.dac.gov.in/

2.8 Paramparagat Krishi Vikas Yojana (PKVY) and natural/organic farming support
Purpose:
Support eligible clusters and groups transitioning to organic or natural farming practices,
including training, local production systems, certification/assurance and market linkage
as provided by current guidelines.

Important:
- Many benefits are delivered through clusters, groups or implementing agencies rather
  than as an unrestricted individual cash payment.
- Certification and labelling rules matter if a farmer wants to sell produce as certified
  organic.
- A farmer should not claim that produce is certified organic merely because chemical
  fertilizer use has been reduced.
- Check the current scheme version, cluster availability, local implementing agency and
  certification/PGS requirements.

2.9 Rashtriya Krishi Vikas Yojana (RKVY) / current RKVY components
Purpose:
Support state-led agricultural development, innovation, infrastructure, productivity,
value addition and allied sectors through approved components and projects.

The exact farmer-facing benefit depends on the component implemented in that state and
district. Do not describe RKVY as one universal cash subsidy. Ask the district agriculture
office which active component is open.

2.10 National Food Security and Nutrition Mission (NFSNM; formerly NFSM)
Purpose:
Improve production and productivity of selected food crops and support demonstrations,
quality seed, improved practices, training and other approved interventions.

Potential crop focus and support can change by year and state. The farmer must check
whether the crop, block and intervention are included in the current district action plan.

2.11 Mission for Integrated Development of Horticulture (MIDH)
Purpose:
Support horticulture development and eligible interventions in fruits, vegetables, spices,
flowers, plantation crops, nurseries, protected cultivation, post-harvest management and
related infrastructure.

Possible assistance areas:
- Quality planting material and nursery development.
- Area expansion and orchard establishment.
- Protected cultivation such as shade-net or polyhouse, where approved.
- Pack houses, grading, storage and post-harvest systems.
- Rejuvenation and other crop-specific horticulture interventions.

Subsidy is component-specific and may require technical standards, prior approval,
inspection and approved planting material or vendors. Check the latest state horticulture
department guidelines before investment.

2.12 National Mission on Edible Oils — Oilseeds / Oil Palm
Purpose:
Increase domestic production of edible oils through eligible seed, cultivation, extension,
processing and value-chain support.

Farmer must check:
- Whether the crop and district are covered.
- Whether seed/planting material and inputs are approved.
- Whether procurement or price-assurance features apply and under what terms.
- The current season's beneficiary and subsidy conditions.

2.13 Formation and Promotion of 10,000 Farmer Producer Organisations (FPOs)
Purpose:
Help farmers aggregate produce, improve input purchasing, access markets, services,
storage, processing and institutional finance through member-owned producer organisations.

How an individual farmer may benefit:
- Join a genuine FPO and understand its membership rules.
- Pool produce for bulk sale and stronger bargaining.
- Access inputs and services through group procurement.
- Explore grading, processing, storage and market contracts.
- Ask for transparent accounts, weighing records, price calculation and payment timelines.

Do not promise that joining an FPO guarantees a higher price or a grant. Explain membership
fees, share capital, governance and commercial risks.

2.14 PM-AASHA — Price support and market intervention
Purpose:
Support farmers under notified procurement or market intervention mechanisms for selected
commodities, subject to government decisions and operational arrangements.

Important:
- Not all crops or all markets are covered at all times.
- Procurement depends on commodity, quality specifications, quantity limits, procurement
  window and designated agency.
- A Minimum Support Price announcement does not automatically mean that every farmer's
  entire crop will be purchased at MSP.
- Verify the current procurement centre, dates, registration, quality standard and payment
  process with the state procurement agency/APMC.

2.15 National Mission on Natural Farming (NMNF)
Purpose:
Promote natural farming systems through clusters, training, demonstrations and locally
adapted practices as implemented under current guidelines.

Do not imply that every natural input is automatically approved or that transition has no
yield risk. Encourage demonstration plots, soil monitoring and gradual transition.

2.16 PM-Kisan Maandhan Yojana (PM-KMY)
Purpose:
A contributory pension scheme for eligible small and marginal farmers, subject to age,
landholding, exclusion and contribution conditions.

Important:
This is a pension/contribution arrangement, not an immediate crop subsidy. The farmer
should obtain the current contribution chart and eligibility rules from the official scheme
or authorised enrolment channel before joining.

2.17 Other central and allied-sector schemes to check
Depending on the farmer's activity, the assistant should also check current official
guidelines for:
- National Livestock Mission (NLM).
- Rashtriya Gokul Mission.
- Animal Husbandry Infrastructure Development Fund (AHIDF).
- National Programme for Dairy Development (NPDD).
- Pradhan Mantri Matsya Sampada Yojana (PMMSY).
- Fisheries and Aquaculture Infrastructure Development Fund (FIDF).
- National Beekeeping and Honey Mission (NBHM).
- Namo Drone Didi, where the eligible women SHG/group structure applies.
- AgriSURE and other eligible agri-startup/rural enterprise financing programmes.
- Agri-Clinics and Agri-Business Centres (ACABC), where eligible.
- Rural livelihood and MGNREGA convergence programmes for eligible community or farm
  works as allowed by state rules.

These are not all direct individual-farmer subsidies. The relevant ministry, bank, group,
enterprise or state department may be the implementing agency.

SECTION 3 — KARNATAKA STATE AGRICULTURE SCHEMES AND SERVICES

Karnataka has agriculture services delivered through the Department of Agriculture,
Horticulture, Animal Husbandry, Fisheries, Sericulture, Cooperation, APMCs, banks and
local bodies. The farmer should first identify the correct department and local office.

3.1 FRUITS — Farmer Registration and Unified Beneficiary Information System
FRUITS is an important farmer registration and beneficiary information system in
Karnataka. It supports farmer identification and access to eligible benefits across
departments.

What the farmer should know:
- FRUITS registration is not itself approval for every scheme.
- A FRUITS ID may be used to connect land and beneficiary information for scheme
  processing.
- The farmer should ensure name, Aadhaar-related identity fields, bank details, land
  records and crop details are accurate.
- If land is inherited, divided, leased or recently mutated, the record may need correction.
- A farmer who does not use a smartphone can request help at a Raitha Samparka Kendra,
  authorised service centre or relevant department office.

Karnataka farmer services portal: https://raitamitra.karnataka.gov.in/
FRUITS portal: https://fruits.karnataka.gov.in/

3.2 Raitha Samparka Kendra (RSK)
RSKs are local agriculture extension and farmer-contact centres, generally organised at
hobli level. They are an important offline first stop for farmers.

A farmer can ask the RSK about:
- Current agriculture department schemes and application windows.
- Farmer registration and FRUITS-related assistance.
- Seed availability and certified seed distribution.
- Soil testing and fertilizer recommendations.
- Crop pest and disease advice.
- Demonstrations, training and extension programmes.
- Eligibility and referral for machinery, irrigation and other subsidies.
- Where to submit documents and how to track an application.
- Which taluk or district officer handles a complaint.

The exact services and stock available at each RSK vary. The assistant should locate the
RSK for the farmer's own hobli, not assume the Bengaluru city office is the correct office.

3.3 Krishi Bhagya Yojane
Purpose:
Support rainfed farming and improve rainwater conservation and use.

Historically documented components have included:
- Farm ponds (Krishi Honda).
- Pond lining or approved water-retention structures.
- Pump sets and water-lifting support where permitted.
- Drip or sprinkler systems.
- Other approved water-conservation components.

Eligibility and subsidy:
- Depends on the current year's scheme guidelines, farmer category, land, district,
  approved design and budget.
- Historical subsidy percentages must not be presented as current guarantees.
- A farmer should obtain approval and technical specifications before constructing a pond
  or purchasing a pump.

3.4 Karnataka farm mechanisation and Krishi Yantra Dhare / custom hiring
Purpose:
Improve access to machinery and reduce dependence on manual labour.

Two different routes should be explained:
A. Individual equipment subsidy: eligible farmers may apply for specified equipment under
   the active farm mechanisation programme.
B. Custom hiring: farmers rent machinery from an approved Custom Hiring Centre or
   Krishi Yantra Dhare facility instead of buying it.

The second route can be especially useful for small farmers who cannot afford a tractor,
harvester or expensive equipment. Ask the RSK or taluk agriculture office for the nearest
active centre, equipment list, rent, availability and booking process.

3.5 Micro-irrigation support in Karnataka
Drip and sprinkler assistance may be implemented through PMKSY Per Drop More Crop and
state horticulture/agriculture programmes.

The percentage and ceiling depend on the current notification, crop, land area, farmer
category and component. Do not reuse old rates from a previous financial year. Obtain
written eligibility and approved supplier details before purchase.

3.6 Karnataka seed distribution and input assistance
The state may distribute eligible certified or high-yielding seeds and selected inputs
through seasonal programmes at subsidised rates.

The farmer should ask:
- Which crop varieties are available for this season?
- Is the seed certified and suitable for this agro-climatic zone?
- What is the beneficiary contribution?
- Is there a per-farmer or per-acre limit?
- Is Aadhaar/FRUITS registration required?
- Where and when will the seed be distributed?

Do not recommend a variety solely because it is subsidised. Match the variety to rainfall,
soil, irrigation, sowing window, market demand and disease resistance.

3.7 Raitha Siri and millet-related incentives
Raitha Siri has been described in Karnataka scheme information as support for farmers
growing selected minor millets. Its exact current status, crop list, amount, land ceiling,
registration and payment conditions must be checked in the current Karnataka government
notification. Do not promise a fixed per-hectare amount based only on an old scheme page.

3.8 Karnataka crop insurance — Raitha Suraksha / PMFBY implementation
Karnataka implements crop insurance arrangements through the notified scheme and
Samrakshane systems for relevant seasons and crops.

Official portal for Karnataka crop insurance: https://samrakshane.karnataka.gov.in/
The national PMFBY portal is: https://pmfby.gov.in/

A farmer should verify:
- Notified village/area.
- Notified crop and season.
- Enrolment deadline.
- Premium amount.
- Insurance company and policy number.
- Loss reporting procedure and current deadline.
- Status and grievance channel.

3.9 Karnataka horticulture schemes
Farmers growing fruit, vegetables, flowers, spices, plantation crops and nursery crops
should contact the Horticulture Department rather than assuming all benefits are handled
by the Agriculture Department.

Possible support components under current horticulture programmes may include:
- Orchard and area expansion.
- Quality planting material.
- Protected cultivation, shade-net and polyhouse.
- Drip irrigation.
- Pack houses, grading, sorting and post-harvest facilities.
- Mushroom cultivation and other approved enterprises.
- Horticulture mechanisation and crop-specific support.

Component, beneficiary share, subsidy cap, approved technical standards and district
availability must be checked in the current annual guidelines.

3.10 Karnataka livestock and allied agriculture support
For dairy, cattle, sheep, goat, piggery, poultry, fisheries, beekeeping or sericulture, the
farmer should contact the corresponding department. Agriculture office staff may refer the
farmer, but should not be treated as the only authority.

Possible schemes to investigate, subject to current status:
- Pashu Bhagya and other state livestock assistance.
- National Livestock Mission.
- Rashtriya Gokul Mission.
- AHIDF and dairy infrastructure support.
- PM Matsya Sampada Yojana for eligible fisheries activities.
- Sericulture department support for eligible silkworm rearing and reeling activities.
- Beekeeping and honey mission components.

A livestock subsidy may be credit-linked and require a bank loan, veterinary inspection,
unit size, insurance or approved project. Explain the loan and subsidy portions separately.

3.11 Karnataka agricultural marketing and market-price services
Farmers can use APMC yards, regulated market channels, FPOs, cooperatives and approved
digital market systems depending on the commodity and location.

Useful official/state resources:
- Karnataka Raitha Mitra: https://raitamitra.karnataka.gov.in/
- Karnataka e-market / Unified Market Platform information should be checked through
  current Department of Agricultural Marketing resources.
- National AGMARKNET market information: https://agmarknet.gov.in/
- e-NAM: https://www.enam.gov.in/

A displayed mandi price is an indicative market report, not a guaranteed sale price.
Confirm grade, quality, arrivals, commission, transport, weighing, deductions and payment
terms before dispatching produce.

3.12 Karnataka farmer grievance and call support
A farmer who cannot use a website should be able to ask a family member, RSK, CSC,
panchayat office, FPO or agriculture extension worker to help. The assistant should
provide a phone-first and offline path.

Some Karnataka government and directory material references a Raitha Call Centre
number 1800 425 3553. Since contact numbers can change, the assistant must verify this
number against the current official Karnataka Department of Agriculture contact page
before presenting it as active. If not verified, direct the farmer to the nearest RSK or
district agriculture office and do not invent a replacement number.

A KVK provides district- and crop-relevant training, demonstrations, diagnostics and
agricultural extension. Farmers should identify the KVK serving their district through the
ICAR/KVK directory or ask the district agriculture office. Do not assume the Bengaluru
GKVK campus is the nearest KVK for a farmer living in another district.

OFFICE VISIT CHECKLIST
Before travelling, the farmer should:
- Call or confirm the office is still at the listed address.
- Ask whether the office handles the farmer's village/taluk.
- Ask which officer or section handles the issue.
- Carry copies, not leave original land deeds or identity documents without a formal
  acknowledgement.
- Carry the scheme name, application number, survey number and relevant crop details.
- Ask for a written acknowledgement for any submitted document.
- Avoid paying unofficial cash. Request an official receipt for any authorised fee.

SECTION 5 — HOW TO SUPPORT FARMERS WHO DO NOT OWN OR USE SMARTPHONES

The platform must never assume that every farmer has a smartphone, mobile data, email,
digital literacy, English fluency or a bank app.

A farmer without a smartphone should still be able to:
- Ask a question through a shared family phone or a village help desk.
- Call a support number where an officially verified number exists.
- Use a basic phone through voice/IVR or SMS if the service supports it.
- Visit the RSK, CSC, panchayat office, cooperative society, FPO or bank.
- Receive a printed application checklist, scheme summary or acknowledgement.
- Ask an extension worker to explain an application in the farmer's preferred language.
- Authorise a trusted helper where the official process permits it, without sharing PINs
  or OTPs.

Recommended product design:
1. Provide a voice-first interface and support local languages such as Kannada, Hindi,
   Telugu, Tamil and other languages relevant to the region.
2. Use short sentences and one question at a time.
3. Read out the key answer and offer a printable or downloadable checklist.
4. Give an offline route: "Visit your nearest Raitha Samparka Kendra / CSC."
5. Let an assisted-service operator search schemes using district, taluk, crop and need.
6. Do not ask for Aadhaar, bank account number, OTP or sensitive documents in ordinary
   chat.
7. Explain that an operator can help submit a form but cannot guarantee approval.
8. Make a clear distinction between an official government office, a private agent and
   the platform's own vendor.
9. Provide a complaint path for suspected fraud or unofficial payment demands.
10. Make it possible to save a case number on paper or by SMS.

Example:
Farmer: "I do not know how to apply online. I have no smartphone."
Assistant: "That is okay. You do not need to complete the process alone. Tell me your
village, taluk and the crop or support you need. I can explain which office to visit and
what documents to carry. In Karnataka, the local Raitha Samparka Kendra is usually a
good first place to ask about agriculture schemes. Do not give anyone your OTP or bank
PIN."

SECTION 6 — SUBSIDY AND GOVERNMENT-SCHEME FRAUD PREVENTION

Common warning signs:
- Someone promises guaranteed approval for a fee.
- Someone asks for an OTP, UPI PIN, ATM PIN or bank password.
- Someone says a subsidy will expire immediately unless money is transferred to a
  personal account.
- A vendor claims to be government-approved but cannot show a valid authorisation.
- A caller asks the farmer to install an unknown screen-sharing or remote-access app.
- An agent asks the farmer to sign a blank form or hand over original land documents.
- A message contains a suspicious link or spelling errors and asks for bank credentials.
- A person promises an impossible subsidy percentage or a fixed cash amount without
  checking land, crop, category and scheme rules.

Safe response:
"Please do not transfer money or share an OTP. Ask for the scheme name, government
order or official notification, authorised office and written receipt. Verify the details
directly with the department, bank or official portal."

The platform must not:
- Claim to be a government department.
- Imply that a vendor is government-authorised without verified evidence.
- Sell a product as mandatory for a subsidy unless the official scheme says so.
- Collect sensitive credentials.
- Promise a sanction or DBT payment.
- Hide vendor commissions or ranking criteria.

SECTION 7 — FARMER QUESTIONS AND ANSWER PATTERNS FOR RAG

Q: "I own one acre. Which subsidy can I get?"
A: "The answer depends on your state, district, crop, irrigation source, farmer category
and the activity you want support for. Are you looking for seeds, drip irrigation,
machinery, a farm pond, horticulture or a crop loan? I can narrow down the relevant
scheme, but the current notification must be checked before applying."

Q: "Can I get a tractor subsidy?"
A: "Possibly, but it depends on the current farm mechanisation scheme, your category,
landholding rules, equipment specification, district allocation and whether applications
are open. Ask the RSK or taluk agriculture office for the current approved list. Do not
buy the tractor before confirming whether prior approval is required."

Q: "I bought a drip system last month. Can I claim subsidy now?"
A: "Do not assume that a retrospective claim is allowed. Many subsidy programmes require
application, inspection or approval before purchase. Keep your invoice and contact the
implementing department immediately. The officer must check the current scheme rules."

Q: "My PM-KISAN payment has not arrived."
A: "Check your beneficiary status, e-KYC, land-seeding and bank validation on the official
PM-KISAN portal. If you cannot use the portal, visit an authorised CSC or local agriculture
office. Do not share your Aadhaar number or OTP in this chat."

Q: "My crop was destroyed by heavy rain. Can I get insurance?"
A: "First we need to check whether your crop, village and season were notified and whether
you enrolled in the policy. Report the loss immediately through the current official
PMFBY/Karnataka channel and follow the policy's reporting deadline. Keep photographs,
policy details and acknowledgement. Compensation is subject to assessment and policy
rules."

Q: "I am a tenant farmer. Can I apply?"
A: "Some schemes and insurance arrangements may recognise cultivators or tenants when
the required documents and state rules permit it; others require land records in a
particular form. Tell me your state and district. We should check the exact scheme rules
and accepted cultivation proof rather than assume you are eligible or ineligible."

Q: "Where is the nearest agriculture office?"
A: "Please tell me your village, hobli and taluk, or your PIN code. The nearest office must
be matched to the land's administrative jurisdiction. If you are in Karnataka, the local
Raitha Samparka Kendra is usually a good first contact."

Q: "The dealer says I must buy his fertilizer to receive a subsidy."
A: "Do not accept that claim without checking the official scheme notification. Ask the
dealer to show the approved product list and written rule. Confirm with the agriculture
officer or RSK. A subsidy should not be used to pressure you into buying an unnecessary
or unsuitable input."

SECTION 10 — RAG RETRIEVAL AND RESPONSE POLICY FOR SCHEMES

When a farmer asks "What schemes are available?", do not return a huge list immediately.
Ask for the most important missing details one at a time:
1. State and district (or village/taluk).
2. Crop or allied activity.
3. Farmer's objective: income support, crop loss, irrigation, machinery, seed, livestock,
   storage, market access or loan.
4. Land ownership/cultivation status and beneficiary category only if relevant to the
   scheme.

Then:
- Retrieve current official sources matching the district, crop, financial year and need.
- Prefer government notifications and department portals over blogs and scheme aggregators.
- State the benefit type clearly: grant, subsidy, loan, interest concession, insurance,
  procurement or training.
- Give amount/rate only when supported by a current official source.
- Explain whether prior approval is required.
- Give the application route and document checklist.
- Provide an offline route for farmers without smartphones.
- Give the local office only when its jurisdiction is known or clearly label it as a
  district-level starting point.
- Mention the date the scheme information was checked.
- If the current year's rate or application window cannot be verified, say:
  "I can explain the scheme, but I cannot confirm the current subsidy rate or whether
  applications are open. Please verify with the local agriculture office before spending
  money."

The assistant must never:
- Guarantee eligibility, subsidy approval, crop insurance payout or procurement.
- Invent a deadline, subsidy rate, office address, phone number or application link.
- Treat a loan as a grant.
- Treat an MSP announcement as a guaranteed purchase.
- Tell a farmer to purchase equipment before confirming the scheme's prior-approval rules.
- Give legal or financial certainty based on an old notification.
- Use the Bengaluru directory for a farmer whose location is elsewhere.
