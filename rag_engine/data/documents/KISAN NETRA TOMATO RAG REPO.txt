	2. Disease overview
Tomato Leaf Mold is a fungal disease that mainly affects tomato leaves.
It is particularly common in protected cultivation, greenhouses and polyhouses where humidity is high and ventilation is inadequate.
The fungus develops on leaf surfaces and can cause yellow patches on the upper side of leaves and olive-green to greyish fungal growth on the lower side.
Severe infection causes leaves to dry and fall, reducing the plant's ability to produce healthy fruits.
3. Symptoms and identification
Early-stage symptoms
* Pale green or yellowish patches appear on the upper leaf surface.
* Small discoloured areas develop between leaf veins.
* Olive-coloured fungal growth may begin appearing on the underside.
* Lower and older leaves are commonly affected first.
Moderate-stage symptoms
* Yellow patches become larger.
* Olive-green or greyish fungal growth becomes more visible underneath leaves.
* Leaves begin curling.
* Affected leaf tissues become dry.
Severe-stage symptoms
* Extensive leaf yellowing.
* Premature leaf fall.
* Reduced photosynthetic activity.
* Poor fruit development due to loss of functional foliage.
* Reduced marketable yield under severe disease pressure.
4. Causal organism
The disease is caused by Passalora fulva.
It is a specialised fungal pathogen of tomato.
It can survive in infected crop debris, volunteer tomato plants and contaminated protected-cultivation environments. In some situations, contaminated seed or planting material may also contribute to introduction.
5. Disease development and transmission
1. Fungal inoculum survives on infected plant material.
2. Spores spread through air currents, water splash and handling.
3. Spores reach susceptible tomato leaves.
4. High humidity favours spore germination and infection.
5. Fungal growth develops, particularly on the lower leaf surface.
6. Additional spores are produced and spread to nearby leaves.

	7. Effects on tomato production
* Reduced leaf area.
* Reduced photosynthesis.
* Premature defoliation.
* Poor fruit development under severe infection.
* Reduced fruit quality and marketable yield.
* Increased crop-protection costs.
8. Prevention and management
Cultural management
* Maintain adequate spacing between plants.
* Improve greenhouse and polyhouse ventilation.
* Remove heavily infected leaves where practical.
* Avoid excessive overhead irrigation.
* Irrigate early in the day where foliage wetting occurs.
* Remove crop residues after harvesting.
* Rotate away from tomato where practical.
Resistant varieties
* Select resistant or tolerant cultivars where available.
* Check resistance claims against the locally prevalent pathogen races.
Chemical management
* Use only registered fungicides recommended for tomato leaf mold.
* Apply according to local disease pressure and label instructions.
* Rotate fungicide modes of action.
* Observe pre-harvest and re-entry intervals.

11. Frequently asked questions
Q1. Which part of the tomato plant is mainly affected?
Leaves, particularly older and lower leaves.
Q2. What is the most characteristic symptom?
Yellow patches on the upper surface and olive-grey fungal growth underneath.
Q3. Does high humidity increase disease risk?
Yes. High humidity and prolonged leaf wetness are important risk factors.
Q4. Can it affect fruit directly?
Fruit infection is uncommon; the main damage occurs through leaf infection and defoliation.

Q1. Which soil is suitable for tomato?
Well-drained loamy and sandy-loam soils with good fertility are generally suitable.

TOMATO TOPIC 03 – EARLY BLIGHT
1. Disease identification
Parameter
	Information
	Disease name
	Tomato Early Blight

	Alternaria solani

	Common identification feature
	Target or bull's-eye appearance
	Major impact

	2. Disease overview
Tomato Early Blight is a common fungal disease affecting tomato crops.
It can occur at different growth stages but is frequently first noticed on older and lower leaves.
The disease produces characteristic brown lesions with concentric rings, resembling a target or bull's-eye.
As the disease progresses, lesions enlarge, merge and cause leaf drying and premature leaf fall.
Severe infection reduces photosynthetic activity and exposes fruits to direct sunlight, potentially resulting in sunscald.
3. Symptoms and visual identification
Early-stage symptoms
* Small dark brown or black spots develop on older leaves.
* Initial lesions are usually circular.
* A yellowish region may develop around the spots.
* Concentric rings may become visible as lesions enlarge.
Moderate-stage symptoms
* Brown spots become larger.
* Target-like concentric rings become prominent.
* Multiple lesions merge.
* Leaves begin yellowing.
* Lower leaves start drying.
Severe-stage symptoms
* Extensive leaf blighting.
* Premature defoliation.
* Stem lesions may develop.
* Fruits may develop dark lesions near the calyx or stem attachment.
* Fruit quality and marketability decline.
4. Causal organism
The disease is caused by Alternaria solani.
The pathogen produces dark, multicellular spores.
It can survive in:
* Infected crop debris.
* Volunteer tomato plants.
* Infected plant material.
* Potentially contaminated seed.
5. Disease development and transmission
1. The fungus survives on infected plant debris and other inoculum sources.
2. Spores develop on infected tissues.
3. Wind and rain splash disperse spores.
4. Spores reach healthy tomato leaves.
5. Infection occurs under favourable environmental conditions.
6. Brown necrotic lesions develop.
7. New spores are produced and spread to nearby plants.
6. Favourable environmental conditions
Parameter

	Temperature

	Humidity

	Rainfall

	Leaf wetness
	Prolonged wetness favours infection
	Plant stress
	Water and nutrient stress can increase susceptibility
	Crop debris
	Provides a survival source
	7. Effects on tomato production
* Loss of healthy leaf area.
* Reduced photosynthesis.
* Premature leaf fall.
* Reduced fruit development under severe infection.
* Fruit sunscald due to canopy loss.
* Reduced marketable yield.
* Increased crop-protection expenditure.
8. Prevention and management
Cultural management
* Use healthy seeds and seedlings.
* Remove infected plant debris.
* Rotate with non-solanaceous crops.
* Maintain adequate plant spacing.
* Use staking where suitable.
* Avoid prolonged leaf wetness.
* Maintain consistent irrigation.
* Remove severely affected lower leaves where practical.
Biological management
* Use locally recommended biological control organisms where appropriate.
* Trichoderma-based products may be used as part of approved preventive programmes.
* Maintain healthy soil and good crop sanitation.
Chemical management
* Registered fungicides may be recommended when disease risk justifies treatment.
* Use only products labelled for tomato and the target disease.
* Rotate fungicide modes of action.
* Follow the label's dose, waiting period and safety instructions.

	2. Disease overview
Tomato Bacterial Spot is an important bacterial disease affecting tomato production.
It produces small, water-soaked lesions on leaves, stems and fruits.
The disease can spread rapidly under warm, humid and rainy conditions.
Severe infection causes leaf damage, defoliation and fruit blemishes, reducing the market value of harvested tomatoes.
3. Symptoms and identification
Early-stage symptoms
* Small water-soaked spots appear on leaves.
* Spots may initially appear greenish or translucent.
* Small brown lesions develop.
* Yellow halos may appear around some lesions.
Moderate-stage symptoms
* Lesions become dark brown or black.
* Multiple lesions develop on individual leaves.
* Spots may become angular or irregular.
* Older leaves begin yellowing.
* Leaf tissue may become damaged and dry.
Severe-stage symptoms
* Extensive leaf spotting.
* Premature leaf fall.
* Stem and petiole lesions.
* Dark, raised and scabby fruit spots.
* Reduced fruit appearance and marketability.
4. Symptoms on different plant parts

	5. Causal organism
Tomato Bacterial Spot is associated with several Xanthomonas species.
The bacterial species complex includes:
1. Xanthomonas euvesicatoria.
2. Xanthomonas vesicatoria.
3. Xanthomonas perforans.
4. Xanthomonas gardneri.
Species identification may require laboratory testing.

3. Rain splash disperses bacteria.
4. Bacteria reach healthy leaves and fruits.
5. Entry occurs through natural openings or wounds.
6. Lesions develop on infected tissues.
7. Further spread occurs through rain, irrigation splash and contaminated handling.

TOMATO TOPIC 05 – ANTHRACNOSE
1. Disease identification

	2. Disease overview
Tomato Anthracnose is an important fungal disease that mainly affects ripe and overripe tomato fruits.
The disease is caused by fungi belonging to the Colletotrichum group.
Infection can occur while fruits are still green, but visible symptoms commonly develop as the fruits approach maturity and ripening.
The disease reduces fruit quality, shortens shelf life and increases post-harvest losses.
Some Colletotrichum species, particularly C. coccodes, can also cause root infections known as black dot root rot.
3. Symptoms and identification
Early-stage symptoms
* Small circular spots appear on fruits.
* Lesions may be slightly sunken.
* Initial spots may be difficult to identify on green fruits.
* Infection may remain latent before ripening.
Moderate-stage symptoms
* Lesions become larger and depressed.
* Dark spots develop at the centre.
* Lesions may develop concentric patterns.
* Small black structures may appear within the affected tissue.
Severe-stage symptoms
* Large sunken fruit lesions develop.
* Infected areas become soft and rotten.
* Salmon-pink or orange spore masses may appear during moist conditions.
* Multiple lesions may merge.
* Fruits become unsuitable for marketing.
4. Symptoms on different plant parts
Plant part
	Possible symptoms
	Fruits
	Circular, sunken, dark lesions
	Leaves
	Occasional necrotic spots depending on species
	Stems
	Localised lesions in some infections
	Roots

	Fruit surface
	Salmon-coloured spore masses under favourable conditions
	5. Causal organism
The main commonly reported causal organism is Colletotrichum coccodes.
Other Colletotrichum species may also cause anthracnose-like symptoms.
The fungi can survive in:
* Infected crop residues.
* Soil.
* Infected plant material.
* Volunteer host plants.
* Contaminated field environments.
6. Disease development and transmission
1. Fungal inoculum survives in soil and infected plant material.
2. Spores are produced on infected tissues.
3. Spores spread through water splash and contact.
4. Infection may occur on developing green fruits.
5. The pathogen may remain latent.
6. Symptoms become visible as fruits ripen.
7. Infected fruits develop sunken lesions and decay.
8. Diseased fruits can contribute to further contamination.

8. Impact on tomato production
* Fruit rot.
* Reduced shelf life.
* Increased post-harvest rejection.
* Reduced marketable yield.
* Transportation losses.
* Increased sorting and storage costs.
* Lower farmer income.
9. Prevention and management

* Rotate with non-host crops.
* Remove infected fruits.
* Remove infected plant debris.
* Maintain proper plant spacing.
* Use staking to prevent fruit-soil contact.
* Maintain proper drainage.
* Avoid overhead irrigation where possible.
Harvest management

* Avoid bruising and skin injury.
* Remove diseased and overripe fruits.
* Use clean harvesting containers.
* Avoid mixing infected fruits with healthy produce.
Post-harvest management
* Maintain hygienic storage areas.
* Use clean crates.
* Sort infected fruits before packaging.
* Avoid excessive moisture during storage.
* Maintain suitable temperature and ventilation.
Chemical management

* Apply preventive measures according to disease risk.
* Follow label instructions and pre-harvest intervals.
* Avoid unnecessary repeated applications.

13. Frequently asked questions
Q1. Which part of the tomato plant is mainly affected?
The fruits, especially ripe and overripe fruits.
Q2. Can green tomatoes be infected?
Yes. Infection can occur while fruits are green, with symptoms developing later during ripening.
Q3. What is the main identifying symptom?
Circular, depressed or sunken lesions on fruits.
Q4. What are the pink or orange spots sometimes seen on infected fruits?
They can be masses of fungal spores produced under moist conditions.
Q5. Why is the disease important after harvesting?
It causes fruit decay, reduces shelf life and increases market rejection.
