MANGO TOPIC 01 – SOOTY MOULD
1. Disease identification
Parameter

	Disease name

	Crop

	Disease category
	Fungal growth associated with insect infestation

	Main associated pests
	Mango hoppers, scales, mealybugs
	Affected parts
	Leaves, petioles, twigs and fruit surfaces
	Primary concern
	Reduced photosynthesis and weakened tree growth
	2. What is Mango Sooty Mould?
Mango Sooty Mould is characterised by a black, soot-like fungal coating on the surface of mango leaves, branches and sometimes fruits.
It is generally a secondary problem caused by honeydew-producing insects. These insects suck sap from the plant and release a sugary substance called honeydew. Fungi grow on this honeydew, creating the black coating.
The fungus usually grows on the plant surface rather than directly invading healthy plant tissue.
3. Symptoms and identification
Early symptoms
* Small black spots or patches appear on leaf surfaces.
* Sticky honeydew may be noticed on leaves.
* Ant activity may increase around affected branches.
* Small insect colonies may appear on leaf undersides.
Moderate symptoms
* Black fungal coating spreads across larger leaf areas.
* Leaves become dirty-looking and sticky.
* Photosynthesis may decrease.
* Leaves may begin to yellow.
Severe symptoms
* Large portions of the canopy may be covered with black fungal growth.
* Leaf function and overall tree vigour may decline.
* Heavy insect infestation can contribute to premature leaf drop and reduced productivity.
* Fruit surfaces may become dirty and lose market appeal.
4. Causes of the disease
The primary cause is the growth of sooty-mould fungi on honeydew.
Important contributing insects include:
1. Mango hoppers.
2. Mealybugs.
3. Scale insects.
4. Other honeydew-producing sap-sucking insects.
5. Disease development and transmission
1. Sap-sucking insects settle on mango leaves and shoots.
2. They feed on plant sap.
3. Sugary honeydew is released onto plant surfaces.
4. Sooty-mould fungal spores land on the honeydew.
5. Fungal growth develops into a black coating.
6. Additional honeydew production allows the coating to spread.
6. Environmental conditions
Parameter
	Effect
	Temperature
	Warm conditions favour insect activity
	Humidity
	Moist conditions can favour fungal growth
	Canopy density
	Dense canopy restricts airflow
	Sunlight
	Shaded conditions can prolong surface moisture
	Insect population
	Higher populations produce more honeydew
	Orchard sanitation
	Poor management encourages pest persistence
	These are general risk factors, not fixed disease thresholds.
7. Impact on mango production
* Reduced photosynthetic activity.
* Reduced plant vigour under heavy infestation.
* Poor appearance of fruits.
* Lower market quality.
* Increased orchard management costs.
8. Prevention and management
Cultural management
* Maintain proper canopy spacing and pruning.
* Remove heavily infested and dead plant material.
* Improve sunlight penetration.
* Maintain orchard sanitation.
* Monitor new flushes regularly.
Biological management
* Conserve ladybird beetles.
* Encourage lacewings and parasitoids.
* Protect natural enemies of mealybugs and scale insects.
* Avoid unnecessary broad-spectrum insecticides.
Pest management
* Identify the honeydew-producing insect.
* Monitor its population.
* Use locally recommended and registered pest-control measures when necessary.
* Manage ants where they protect scale insects or mealybugs.
9. Farmer action plan

	10. Important precautions
* Do not treat only the black fungal coating.
* Control the underlying insect infestation.
* Avoid unnecessary chemical spraying.
* Follow pesticide label instructions.
* Observe pre-harvest intervals.
* Protect pollinators and beneficial insects.
11. Frequently asked questions
Q1. Is Sooty Mould a fungal disease?
Yes. It is a fungal growth that develops on honeydew deposited by insects.
Q2. Does it directly kill mango trees?
Usually not. However, severe infestations can reduce photosynthesis and weaken tree vigour.
Q3. Can the black coating be removed?
Superficial growth may be washed off, but it can return if honeydew-producing insects are not controlled.
Q4. What is the most important management step?
Identifying and managing the insects responsible for producing honeydew.
MANGO TOPIC 02 – POWDERY MILDEW
1. Disease identification
Parameter
	Information
	Disease name

	2. Disease overview
Mango Powdery Mildew is an important fungal disease affecting mango orchards, particularly during flowering and early fruit development.
The fungus produces white or grey powder-like growth on plant surfaces. It damages flowers and young fruits, resulting in poor fruit setting and reduced yield.
It is particularly important because infection during flowering can significantly affect the number of fruits produced.
3. Symptoms
Early-stage symptoms
* Small white powdery patches appear on leaves.
* White fungal growth develops on flower panicles.
* Young leaves may show distortion.
* Tender floral tissues become infected.
Moderate-stage symptoms
* Powdery growth spreads across panicles.
* Flowers begin drying and turning brown.
* Flower stalks become weakened.
* Small fruitlets may begin dropping.
Severe-stage symptoms
* Entire flower panicles may become infected.
* Flowers dry before successful pollination and fertilisation.
* Young fruits drop prematurely.
* Fruit production can decrease substantially.
4. Causes and infection cycle
The disease is caused by Oidium mangiferae.
1. Fungal spores develop on infected plant tissues.
2. Spores become airborne.
3. They reach susceptible mango leaves, flowers and panicles.
4. Spores germinate and establish infection on plant surfaces.
5. White powdery fungal growth develops.
6. New spores spread to nearby susceptible tissues.
5. Environmental conditions
Parameter
	Disease relationship
	Temperature
	Moderate temperatures can favour infection
	Humidity
	Suitable humidity and dew favour disease development
	Rainfall
	Heavy rain is not necessary for infection
	Dew
	Can support spore germination
	Canopy
	Dense canopy can increase disease pressure
	Crop stage
	Flowering is particularly vulnerable
	6. Effect on mango production
* Reduced flower viability.
* Poor fruit setting.
* Premature fruit drop.
* Reduced fruit numbers per panicle.
* Lower overall marketable yield.
* Increased production costs.
7. Prevention and management
Cultural practices
* Maintain an open orchard canopy.
* Prune overcrowded branches after harvest.
* Remove severely affected panicles where practical.
* Avoid excessive nitrogen fertilisation.
* Maintain balanced tree nutrition.
Monitoring
* Start monitoring when flower panicles emerge.
* Inspect flowers and young fruitlets regularly.
* Increase surveillance during favourable weather.
* Record the percentage of affected panicles.
Chemical management
* Sulphur-based fungicides and other registered fungicides may be recommended under local crop-protection guidelines.
* Apply only when disease pressure and crop stage justify treatment.
* Follow the registered product label and local recommendations.
* Rotate fungicide modes of action to reduce resistance risk.
8. Farmer action checklist
1. Inspect flower panicles regularly.
2. Identify white powdery growth early.
3. Remove heavily infected panicles where feasible.
4. Avoid excessive nitrogen application.
5. Maintain canopy ventilation.
6. Obtain local agricultural advice before spraying.
7. Monitor fruit setting after flowering.
9. Frequently asked questions
Q1. Which stage is most vulnerable?
Flowering and early fruit development.
Q2. Does the disease affect mango fruits?
Yes. Young fruits can become infected and drop prematurely.
Q3. Can it spread without heavy rainfall?
Yes. Powdery mildew does not require prolonged rainfall or heavy leaf wetness.
Q4. What is the primary management objective?
Protect healthy panicles and flowers during flowering and fruit setting.

	MANGO TOPIC 04 – GALL MIDGE
1. Disease and pest identification
Parameter
	Information
	Name
	Mango Gall Midge
	Crop
	Mango
	Scientific crop name
	Mangifera indica
	Pest category
	Insect
	Insect family
	Cecidomyiidae
	Important genera
	Procontarinia, Erosomyia
	Affected parts
	Leaves, shoots, panicles and sometimes young fruits
	Damaging stage
	Larval stage
	Primary impact
	Leaf deformation, panicle damage and fruit loss
	2. Overview
Mango Gall Midge is an insect pest problem caused by tiny fly-like insects.
Adult midges lay eggs on susceptible mango tissues. The larvae feed on developing plant tissues and may cause abnormal swelling, galls, tunnels and deformation.
Different gall-midge species attack different parts of mango trees. Therefore, correct identification is important before selecting management measures.
3. Symptoms
Early symptoms
* Small raised spots on young leaves.
* Tiny swellings or blister-like formations.
* Small puncture marks.
* Abnormal development of tender tissues.
Moderate symptoms
* Development of visible leaf galls.
* Curling and deformation of leaves.
* Small tunnels within affected panicles.
* Browning of damaged plant tissues.
Severe symptoms
* Drying of inflorescences.
* Premature dropping of affected flowers.
* Drying and deformation of tender shoots.
* Reduced fruit setting.
* Reduced production where infestation is extensive.
4. Causes and life cycle
The pest belongs to the gall-midge group of small flies.
Its general life cycle includes:
1. Adult female lays eggs on susceptible plant tissues.
2. Eggs hatch into tiny larvae.
3. Larvae feed on developing tissues.
4. Galls or abnormal growth may develop.
5. Mature larvae leave the affected tissues in species that pupate outside the plant.
6. Pupation and adult emergence follow.
The precise life cycle and pupation location depend on the species.
5. Environmental factors
* Availability of tender leaves and panicles.
* Seasonal emergence of adult midges.
* Suitable humidity.
* Orchard sanitation.
* Local rainfall and soil conditions.
* Presence of susceptible mango varieties.
6. Damage to mango production
* Reduced healthy leaf development.
* Deformation of shoots.
* Damage to flower panicles.
* Poor fruit setting.
* Premature fruitlet drop.
* Reduced marketable production.
7. Management strategies
Cultural management
* Remove and destroy heavily infested leaves and panicles where feasible.
* Remove fallen affected plant material.
* Maintain orchard cleanliness.
* Prune damaged shoots.
* Monitor new flushes and panicle development.
Mechanical management
* Inspect affected tissues.
* Collect representative galls for identification.
* Remove severely affected plant material.
* Avoid spreading infested debris between orchard blocks.
Biological management
* Conserve natural enemies.
* Avoid unnecessary broad-spectrum insecticide applications.
* Maintain biodiversity within the orchard where compatible with commercial production.
Chemical management
* Identify the particular gall-midge species first.
* Determine the vulnerable life-cycle stage.
* Use only currently registered products recommended for mango and the identified pest.
* Follow local KVK and horticulture department recommendations.
8. Monitoring parameters
Parameter
	What to monitor
	Leaf gall incidence
	Percentage of affected leaves
	Panicle damage
	Percentage of affected panicles
	Shoot damage
	Number of damaged shoots
	Larval presence
	Larvae within galls or tunnels
	Pest activity
	Adult emergence where monitored
	Crop impact
	Fruit-set reduction
	9. Farmer action plan
1. Inspect newly developed leaves.
2. Check panicles during flowering.
3. Identify galls and abnormal tissue.
4. Collect samples for expert identification.
5. Remove heavily infested plant parts where practical.
6. Maintain orchard sanitation.
7. Monitor new growth after management.
10. Frequently asked questions
Q1. What causes mango gall formation?
Feeding and tissue interactions associated with certain gall-inducing insects, including gall midges.
Q2. Does gall midge affect flowers?
Yes. Certain species attack mango inflorescences and can damage flower panicles.
Q3. Are all mango galls caused by gall midges?
No. Other insects, mites and physiological conditions can also produce abnormal growth.
Q4. Which stage should farmers monitor?
Tender leaf flushes and developing flower panicles.
MANGO TOPIC 05 – DIEBACK
1. Disease identification
Parameter
	Information
	Disease name
	Mango Dieback
	Crop
	Mango
	Scientific name
	Mangifera indica
	Disease category
	Primarily fungal branch and twig disease
	Common causal organism
	Lasiodiplodia theobromae
	Other possible pathogens
	Botryosphaeriaceae fungi
	Affected parts
	Twigs, branches and shoots
	Main symptom
	Progressive drying from shoot tips
	2. Overview
Mango Dieback is a serious branch and twig disease characterised by progressive drying of plant tissues.
The disease often begins at the terminal end of a branch and progresses towards the main stem.
Infected branches gradually lose their leaves and become dry. Severe infection can affect major branches and reduce orchard productivity.
3. Symptoms
Early-stage symptoms
* Drying of shoot tips.
* Yellowing or browning of leaves.
* Wilting of terminal shoots.
* Small areas of bark discolouration.
Moderate-stage symptoms
* Drying progresses down the branch.
* Leaves turn brown and may remain attached temporarily.
* Bark may develop cracks.
* Internal wood may show brown discolouration.
Severe-stage symptoms
* Entire branches become dry.
* Larger woody tissues may become infected.
* Extensive canopy damage occurs.
* Tree vigour and productivity decline.
4. Causal organism
A commonly associated fungus is Lasiodiplodia theobromae.
It can survive in infected woody tissues and dead branches.
The fungus may enter through:
* Pruning wounds.
* Mechanical injuries.
* Cracks in bark.
* Damaged plant tissues.
* Infected planting material.
Other fungi can also cause dieback-like symptoms, so laboratory identification may be necessary.
5. Disease development
1. Fungal inoculum survives in infected branches.
2. Spores are produced on infected tissues.
3. Spores spread through rain splash and other local dispersal mechanisms.
4. Spores reach wounds or susceptible plant tissues.
5. Infection develops within woody tissues.
6. Shoot tips begin drying.
7. Infection may progress towards larger branches.
6. Favourable conditions
* High humidity.
* Rainy conditions.
* Wounds caused by pruning.
* Poor orchard sanitation.
* Drought stress.
* Waterlogging.
* Weak or poorly maintained trees.
7. Impact on production
* Loss of productive branches.
* Reduced canopy development.
* Reduced flowering potential.
* Declining tree vigour.
* Reduced fruit production.
* Increased orchard maintenance costs.
8. Prevention and management
Cultural management
* Prune infected branches below the visibly affected region and into healthy wood.
* Remove and safely dispose of infected branches.
* Avoid unnecessary mechanical injury.
* Maintain balanced irrigation.
* Avoid waterlogging.
* Maintain proper orchard sanitation.
Pruning sanitation
* Clean pruning tools before use.
* Disinfect tools between affected trees.
* Avoid carrying infected plant debris through the orchard.
* Perform pruning during suitable dry weather.
Chemical management
* Copper-based protectants or other approved wound-management products may be recommended in certain local management programmes.
* Use only products approved for the intended crop and application.
* Chemical applications should supplement sanitation and pruning, not replace them.

	10. Frequently asked questions
Q1. What is the main symptom of mango dieback?
Progressive drying beginning from the shoot tip and extending towards the branch.
Q2. Can pruning control dieback?
Pruning can remove infected branches and reduce inoculum when performed correctly.
Q3. Can the disease spread through pruning tools?
Contaminated tools can contribute to the spread of pathogens and should be cleaned and disinfected.
Q4. Can a severely affected tree recover?
Recovery depends on the extent of infection, the health of the remaining canopy and whether major woody tissues are affected.

	2. Overview
Mango Bacterial Canker is a bacterial disease that affects several parts of the mango tree.
It can produce dark spots on leaves, raised lesions on twigs and branches, and characteristic blemishes on fruits.
Severe infection can affect fruit quality and reduce the market value of harvested mangoes.
3. Symptoms
Leaf symptoms
* Small water-soaked lesions.
* Dark brown or black spots.
* Angular or irregular lesions.
* Yellow halos around certain lesions.
* Drying and dropping of severely affected leaves.
Stem and branch symptoms
* Raised lesions.
* Bark cracking.
* Canker formation.
* Discolouration of affected tissues.
* Sometimes gummy or bacterial exudation.
Fruit symptoms
* Small dark spots.
* Raised lesions on fruit surfaces.
* Cracking of affected areas.
* Reduced external appearance.
* Poor market quality.
4. Causal organism
The disease is associated with Xanthomonas citri pv. mangiferaeindicae.
It is a bacterial plant pathogen.
Bacteria can survive in infected plant tissues and spread through:
* Rain splash.
* Wind-driven rain.
* Contaminated tools.
* Infected planting material.
* Mechanical injuries.
5. Disease development
1. Bacteria survive in infected plant material.
2. Rain and wind-driven water disperse bacteria.
3. Bacteria reach healthy leaves, shoots or fruits.
4. Entry occurs through natural openings or wounds.
5. Infection develops in susceptible plant tissues.
6. Dark lesions and cankers become visible.
7. Further spread occurs under favourable wet conditions.
6. Favourable conditions
* Warm and humid weather.
* Frequent rainfall.
* Prolonged surface wetness.
* Storms and wind-driven rain.
* Mechanical injuries.
* Contaminated pruning equipment.
7. Effects on mango production
* Reduced leaf health.
* Branch damage.
* Fruit blemishes.
* Reduced marketability.
* Increased orchard management costs.
* Potential losses when infection is severe.
8. Management strategies
Preventive practices
* Use healthy nursery plants.
* Use disease-free propagation material.
* Maintain orchard sanitation.
* Avoid unnecessary injuries.
* Avoid working in wet foliage.
* Clean pruning equipment.
Cultural management
* Remove infected fallen material.
* Prune severely affected branches where appropriate.
* Improve canopy airflow.
* Maintain proper drainage.
* Avoid excessive overhead irrigation.
Chemical management
* Certain copper-based protectants may be included in preventive programmes where registered and recommended.
* These are protectants, not reliable cures for established internal infections.
* Do not use antibiotics or homemade chemical mixtures without official authorisation.

	10. Frequently asked questions
Q1. Is bacterial canker caused by a fungus?
No. It is a bacterial disease.
Q2. Can it affect mango fruits?
Yes. It can cause dark, raised and cracked fruit lesions.

MANGO TOPIC 07 – ANTHRACNOSE
1. Disease identification
Parameter
	Information
	Disease name
	Mango Anthracnose
	Crop
	Mango
	Scientific name
	Mangifera indica
	Disease category
	Fungal disease
	Main causal organism
	Colletotrichum gloeosporioides species complex
	Affected parts
	Leaves, flowers, panicles, twigs and fruits
	Main symptoms
	Dark spots, blossom blight and fruit rot
	Major concern
	Pre-harvest and post-harvest losses
	2. Overview
Mango Anthracnose is one of the most economically important fungal diseases affecting mango production.
It affects leaves, flowers, panicles, twigs and fruits.
The disease is particularly damaging during flowering and fruit development. It can also cause significant post-harvest losses.
An important characteristic is latent infection: fruits can become infected while still developing on the tree, but visible symptoms may appear only when the fruit ripens.
3. Symptoms
Leaf symptoms
* Small brown or black spots.
* Irregular necrotic patches.
* Spots may merge into larger lesions.
* Severe infection can cause leaf drying and premature leaf fall.
Flower symptoms
* Dark spots on flower panicles.
* Browning and drying of flowers.
* Blossom blight.
* Reduced fruit setting.
Twig symptoms
* Small necrotic lesions.
* Drying of young shoots.
* Localised twig blight.
Fruit symptoms
* Small dark spots on fruit skin.
* Circular or irregular lesions.
* Sunken black patches.
* Lesions enlarge as fruits ripen.
* Softening and rotting of infected fruit.
* Salmon-pink spore masses may sometimes develop under humid conditions.
4. Causal organism
The disease is caused by fungi belonging to the Colletotrichum species complex.
A commonly reported species is Colletotrichum gloeosporioides.
These fungi can survive on:
* Infected leaves.
* Dead branches.
* Diseased panicles.
* Infected fruits.
* Mummified plant material.
5. Disease development and transmission
1. Fungal pathogens survive on infected plant debris.
2. Spores develop on infected tissues.
3. Rain splash and wind-driven rain spread spores.
4. Spores reach healthy leaves, flowers or fruits.
5. Infection develops under favourable conditions.
6. Young fruits may carry latent infections.
7. Symptoms become visible during ripening and storage.
6. Environmental conditions

Yes. It causes blossom blight and can reduce fruit setting.
Q2. Can healthy-looking mangoes carry anthracnose?

	2. Overview
Mango Stem-End Rot is an important post-harvest disease affecting mango fruits.
The disease usually begins around the stem attachment or stem scar and progresses towards the internal fruit flesh.
The infection may develop during fruit growth and remain latent until harvest. Visible symptoms commonly appear during ripening, storage and transportation.
It is particularly important for farmers involved in commercial mango marketing and export.
3. Symptoms
Early-stage symptoms
* Brown discolouration around the stem end.
* Small dark patches near the stem scar.
* Initial softening around the affected area.
Moderate-stage symptoms
* Brown or black rot spreads from the stem end.
* Internal fruit flesh becomes discoloured.
* Softening increases.
* The affected area expands during ripening.
Severe-stage symptoms
* Extensive internal fruit decay.
* Dark brown or black pulp.
* Watery or soft fruit tissues.
* Unpleasant odour may develop.
* Fruits become unsuitable for marketing.
4. Causal organisms
The disease is commonly associated with Lasiodiplodia theobromae and other fungi belonging to the Botryosphaeriaceae family.
These fungi may survive in:
* Dead branches.
* Infected twigs.
* Dead panicles.
* Infected plant debris.
* Infected orchard tissues.
5. Disease development
1. Fungal pathogens survive in infected orchard material.
2. They colonise susceptible plant tissues.
3. Fruit infection may occur before harvesting.
4. Infection may remain latent during fruit development.
5. Fungi become active during fruit ripening.
6. Rot develops around the stem end.
7. Internal fruit tissues become damaged.
6. Favourable conditions
* Warm temperatures.
* High relative humidity.
* Poor orchard sanitation.
* Infected branches and plant debris.
* Harvesting injuries.
* Improper fruit handling.
* Contaminated crates.
* Unsuitable storage conditions.

11. Frequently asked questions
Q1. Why does mango rot start near the stem?
Certain fungi infect or colonise tissues around the stem attachment and subsequently spread into the fruit.
Q2. Does the disease appear only after harvesting?
No. Infection can occur before harvesting, but symptoms often become visible during ripening.
Q3. Can stem-end rot be confused with anthracnose?
Yes. Both cause fruit decay, but their typical starting locations and symptom patterns differ.
Q4. Can washing prevent the disease completely?
No. Washing and sanitation can reduce contamination but cannot eliminate latent internal infections.
Q5. Is it important for mango exporters?
Yes. Post-harvest decay can lead to shipment rejection, reduced shelf life and financial losses.
